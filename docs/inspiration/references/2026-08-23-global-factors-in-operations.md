## Definition

Four global factors present opportunities to an operations manager:

- **global sourcing**
- **economies of scale**
- **scanning and learning**
- **research and development (R&D)**

Together they are how globalisation reaches the operations function, which makes
this note serve both ops-01 (as a strategy) and ops-02 (as the impact of
globalisation).

## Explanation

**Global sourcing.** Purchasing supplies or services without being constrained
by location. As an operations strategy it is broader than the supply chain
version: it is any decision that pursues a cost advantage by exposing the choice
to the global market rather than the domestic one. What that exposure tests the
options against is worth listing, because it is more than price: cost,
efficiency, productivity, technical ability, and the ability to operate over
more hours of the day.

Its challenges are the supply chain ones plus two the source adds here.
**Financial**: exchange rate fluctuation is a risk carried on every
international contract. **Contractual**: language and cultural variation,
differing regulation, and misunderstanding created by a poorly negotiated
service level agreement. That second one puts the weight where it belongs, on
the quality of the contract rather than on the decision to sign one.

**Economies of scale.** Cost advantages from increasing the size of production,
so that cost per unit falls and profitability can rise. It becomes a *global*
factor for a simple structural reason: any single nation has a limited
population to sell to, so a business that needs volume has to go outside it.
Selling globally and sourcing globally then become the same strategic decision
seen from two ends.

Scale economies are not only in production. They arise in capital investment and
better use of technology; in marketing, where a multinational's branding and
advertising avoid duplication across markets; and in human resources, where
training and development programs are applied across the whole business rather
than rebuilt per country.

**Scanning and learning.** Watching what other businesses do, anywhere in the
world, and adapting it. Also called environmental scanning. The channels are
management journals, industry associations, conferences, and staff and managers
who bring experience from other businesses and other countries. Much of
contemporary practice arrived this way: the post-war Japanese emphasis on
quality, care and continuous improvement, **kaizen**, was learned by everyone
else rather than invented by them.

The competitive dynamic in the source's supermarket case is the useful part: one
business invests in self-scan checkouts and a competitor follows with an improved
version; a third builds strong supplier relationships that force the others to
rebuild their supply chain management. Scanning is cheap, which means an
advantage that can be observed will be copied, and copied with the benefit of
seeing what went wrong the first time.

**Research and development.** Allocating resources to create leading edge
technology and innovative products. A central part of it is establishing what
consumers want and building to meet it, so R&D is not purely technical.
Government supports it with tax concessions and grants because the private
return is smaller than the national one.

The source makes an argument rather than only a description here, and flags it
as such: R&D should contribute more to Australian business decision making than
it currently does, with cuts to the incentive scheme a concern and Australian
investment below Europe, China, the United States, South Korea and Japan.

## Relationships to other concepts

The four factors are not parallel. Two lower cost, two raise capability, and
saying which is which is the structure of a good ops-02 answer:

| factor | what it acts on | the objective it serves |
| --- | --- | --- |
| global sourcing | inputs | cost, and quality where the best supplier is overseas |
| economies of scale | volume | cost per unit |
| scanning and learning | practice | quality, speed, dependability, by adopting what works elsewhere |
| research and development | capability | quality of design, customisation, and future cost |

The trade-offs the source does not state:

- **Economies of scale buy cost with flexibility.** High volume standardised
  production is the least adaptable configuration a business can have. Scale and
  customisation are close to opposites, and a business pursuing both needs mass
  customisation to reconcile them.
- **Scanning and learning is available to competitors too.** It is the one
  strategy with no barrier to imitation, so it produces parity rather than
  advantage. The business that scans best moves first, which is a timing
  advantage, not a durable one.
- **R&D is a bet.** It is spending now against an uncertain return, which is
  exactly why government has to subsidise it. It is also the only one of the
  four that can produce an advantage competitors cannot simply copy.
- **Global sourcing trades dependability for cost**, and adds exchange rate risk
  that has nothing to do with operations and can undo the saving entirely.

For ops-02, which asks for an assessment of globalisation's impact on
operations, this set gives both sides. Globalisation widens the supplier pool,
enlarges the market enough to justify scale, spreads best practice, and raises
the R&D standard a business must meet. It also lengthens supply lines, exposes
the business to foreign currency and foreign regulation, hands competitors the
same access, and makes the whole operation more complex to manage.

## Terminology

- **global sourcing** — sourcing supplies or services without being constrained
  by location
- **economies of scale** — cost advantages from increasing the scale of
  production, lowering cost per unit
- **multinational corporation (MNC)** — a business operating in more than one
  country
- **scanning and learning** — observing global best practice and adapting it;
  also environmental scanning
- **kaizen** — Japanese for improvement; continuous improvement across every
  level of a business
- **best practice** — the most effective known way of performing an activity
- **research and development (R&D)** — resources allocated to creating new
  technology, products and solutions
- **service level agreement (SLA)** — the level of service a customer expects
  from a supplier, the metrics measuring it, and the remedies if it is not met
- **exchange rate fluctuation** — movement in a currency's value, which changes
  the domestic cost of an overseas contract

## Distinctions and confusions

- **Global sourcing here is broader than in supply chain management.** In supply
  chain management it is procurement of inputs. As a global factor it covers any
  decision taken against the global market for a cost advantage, including
  outsourcing.
- **Economies of scale are not just discounts.** They arise in capital,
  technology, marketing and human resources as well as in production volume.
- **Scale is not size.** A large business with a poorly used plant has no
  economies of scale. Scale economies are about cost per unit, not headcount.
- **Scanning and learning is not copying.** It is adapting practice to a
  different business and market. Practices transplanted without adaptation
  frequently fail, which is why the second mover often improves on the first.
- **R&D is not the same as innovation.** R&D is the spending. Innovation is the
  result, and it is not guaranteed.
- **A global factor is an opportunity and a pressure.** The same globalisation
  that opens a supplier pool opens the domestic market to competitors who found
  it first. Answers that treat globalisation as a menu the business chooses from
  have taken half the question.

## Worked example, structurally

1. name the global factor and what the business actually did
2. name the performance objective, and be precise about which
3. state the mechanism, and make it specific to being global: what does
   crossing a border make possible that a domestic version would not
4. name the exposure it created: currency, distance, regulation, complexity, or
   competitors gaining the same access
5. judge whether the gain was durable, and say why or why not

Step 3 is where most answers fail, because the reasoning given would work just
as well for a domestic supplier. Step 5 matters because two of these four
factors produce advantages that decay: scanning is copied, and scale is matched.

## What the source does not give us

- No trade-offs at all. Four factors are presented as four opportunities, and
  only global sourcing is given challenges.
- Nothing on the durability of the advantage each produces, though scanning and
  learning is defined in a way that makes its imitability obvious.
- No link from any of the four to the named performance objectives.
- The R&D comparison figures are quoted from an index without a date or a
  method, so they are background for authoring and not usable as student
  evidence.
- Economies of scale get no counterweight: no diseconomies, no lost flexibility,
  no account of what a high-volume standardised operation gives up.
