## Concepts (SOURCE)

Five objectives of financial management:

| objective | definition | horizon |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **profitability** | the ability to maximise profit, the excess of revenue over expenses | both |
| **growth** | the ability to increase size over the longer term | long |
| **efficiency** | minimising costs and managing assets so maximum profit comes from the lowest possible level of assets | both |
| **liquidity** | the ability to meet financial commitments in the short term, under twelve months | short |
| **solvency** | the ability to meet financial commitments in the longer term, beyond twelve months | long |

Financial management's job is to decide how best to achieve them: identifying
and evaluating alternative courses of action and recommending one.

## Explanation (SOURCE)

**Profitability** is maximising profit. Achieving it means monitoring four
things: revenue and pricing policy, costs and expenses, inventory levels, and
levels of assets. Profit satisfies owners and shareholders now and underwrites
the business's survival later, so it is not purely a short-term objective.

**Growth** is increasing the size of the business over time, and it depends on
developing and using the asset structure to raise sales, profit and market
share. Growth is what makes a business sustainable into the future, which is why
it counts as a financial objective rather than only an ambition.

**Efficiency** is the objective students define worst. It is not "working
harder" or "lower costs" alone. It is the *ratio*: maximum profit from the
lowest possible level of assets. It attaches to the revenue-producing activity of
the business, and achieving it needs control measures that monitor inventory,
cash and the collection of receivables. Two businesses with identical profit can
differ enormously in efficiency if one needed twice the assets to get there.

**Liquidity** is meeting commitments within twelve months. It needs either
enough cash flow, or current assets convertible to cash quickly, such as
inventory. The point worth teaching is that liquidity has *two* failure modes,
not one: a cash shortfall and excess idle cash both cost the business
profitability. Liquidity is a target range, not a maximum.

**Solvency** is meeting commitments over the longer term, and it matters most to
owners, shareholders and creditors because it measures the risk to their money.
It answers whether the business can repay what it borrowed to invest in capital,
such as equipment, machinery or premises. It is measured by **gearing**, the
proportion of assets funded from outside the business, which is to say the
business's reliance on external finance.

**Short term and long term.** Financial objectives come from the strategic plan
and split by horizon:

- **short term** — tactical plans, one to two years, and operational plans,
  day to day. Reviewed regularly against targets and against whether resources
  are being used to best advantage.
- **long term** — strategic plans, generally beyond five years. Broad goals such
  as raising profit or market share, each of which needs a series of short-term
  goals to reach it, and reviewed annually.

**Conflicts between horizons.** This is the part the syllabus explicitly asks
students to explain, and it is the most examinable idea in the topic.

Many objectives complement each other. Some do not, and the pattern is always
the same shape: **the thing that improves the long term consumes cash now.**

- *Growth against short-term profitability.* Expansion carries increased costs
  and increased gearing, which lowers profit in the short term. Managers,
  employees, suppliers and the community favour it; owners, shareholders and
  investors may not, because they carry the near-term cost.
- *Research and development against short-term profitability.* Expensive, slow,
  and possibly years from a result. Bad in the short run, potentially very good
  in the long run.
- *Investment against liquidity.* Long-term profitability requires investment in
  human and physical resources, and money committed to those is money not
  available to meet short-term obligations.

The source draws the right conclusion: short-term thinking by managers can
discourage the long-term investment that would have produced greater profit. The
financial manager's job is to assess achievement across all five and satisfy as
many as possible, which is an explicit statement that they cannot all be
maximised at once.

## Relationships and mechanisms (DERIVED unless marked)

These five are the **effect** end of every financial management argument. The
strategies — cash flow management, working capital management, profitability
management, global financial management — are the **cause** end. That is exactly
the shape of fin-01, "how can financial strategies affect the objectives of
financial management", and it means a paragraph needs strategy vocabulary on one
side and objective vocabulary on the other.

The objectives themselves compete, and naming which pairs compete is what turns
a description into an argument:

- **liquidity against profitability.** Cash held to stay liquid is cash earning
  nothing. Idle cash is a stated cost, not a safety margin.
- **solvency against growth.** Growth funded by debt raises gearing, which is
  precisely what lowers solvency. A business can grow itself into insolvency.
- **efficiency against liquidity.** Efficiency means the lowest possible level of
  assets, and current assets are what liquidity is made of. Squeezing inventory
  and receivables improves efficiency and reduces the buffer.
- **growth against short-term profitability.** Stated by the source.
- **efficiency against growth.** Growth usually means acquiring assets before the
  revenue they generate arrives, which worsens the profit-to-assets ratio in the
  interim.

Solvency and liquidity are not the same objective at different speeds, though
students treat them that way. Liquidity is about whether the cash arrives in
time. Solvency is about whether the business is financed in a way it can
survive. A business can be liquid and insolvent, and it can be solvent and run
out of cash.

Financial objectives also connect out: efficiency depends on operations
(inventory levels are an operations decision), profitability depends on
marketing (pricing and revenue), and growth depends on human resources
(capability to run a larger business).

## Terminology (SOURCE)

- **profitability** — the excess of revenue or income over expenses or costs, and
  the ability to maximise it
- **growth** — the ability to increase the size of the business over the longer
  term
- **efficiency** — achieving maximum profit from the lowest possible level of
  assets
- **liquidity** — the ability to meet financial commitments in under twelve
  months
- **solvency** — the ability to meet commitments beyond twelve months
- **gearing** — the proportion of debt to equity financing the business; the
  measure of solvency and of reliance on external finance
- **receivables** — money owed to the business by its customers
- **idle cash** — cash held but not working, which costs profitability
- **strategic plan** — the long-term plan, generally beyond five years
- **tactical plan** — the one to two year plan
- **operational plan** — the day-to-day plan

## Distinctions and confusions

- **Liquidity is not solvency.** Under twelve months against beyond twelve
  months, and cash timing against financing structure. This is the single most
  commonly confused pair in the finance topic.
- **Efficiency is not cost minimisation.** It is profit relative to assets
  employed. A business can cut costs and become less efficient if it also loses
  revenue.
- **More liquidity is not better.** Excess idle cash costs profitability. The
  objective is sufficiency, not maximisation, and saying so distinguishes a
  strong answer.
- **Growth is not profit.** A business can grow revenue and market share while
  becoming less profitable, and often does during expansion.
- **Gearing is not debt.** It is the proportion of debt to total funding. A large
  debt in a large business may be low gearing.
- **Short-term objectives are not lesser objectives.** They are the mechanism by
  which the long-term ones are reached. Long-term goals each require a series of
  short-term goals.
- **The five cannot all be maximised.** The source says the manager satisfies as
  many as possible. An answer that treats them as independent targets has missed
  the topic.

## Instructional implications (USE)

1. name the objective, and use its precise definition rather than its everyday
   sense
2. name the strategy or decision acting on it
3. state the mechanism: which number moves, in which direction, and why
4. name the objective it costs, and the horizon on which the cost falls
5. judge whether the trade suited this business's position and stage

Step 4 carries the marks on any finance judgement question, because every
financial decision has a counterparty objective. Step 5 is where the short term
against long term conflict belongs: the same decision is right for a business
with cash and wrong for one without.
