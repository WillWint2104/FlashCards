## Definition

**Inventory** (stock) is the quantity of raw materials, work in progress and
finished goods a business holds at a point in time. **Inventory management** is
the set of decisions about how much of it to hold, when it arrives, and how it
is valued at the end of an accounting period.

Every business holds some stock. The strategy question is never whether to hold
stock but how much, and that question is a trade-off with an answer that differs
by business.

## Explanation

**Why hold stock.** Holding stock is fundamentally a risk reduction strategy. It
lets the business meet demand the moment it appears, which keeps the customer
from buying from a competitor. It shortens lead time between order and delivery.
It allows an alternative to be offered when a line runs out, so the sale is
converted rather than lost. Stock can be pushed to distribution centres and
moved to customers as required, and a reserve lets a business open a new market
it could not otherwise supply. Older stock can be discounted, which raises cash
and can pull through sales of other goods. Buying in bulk earns economies of
scale on inputs. Stock is also an asset with a value on the balance sheet.

**Why not hold stock.** Holding costs run to storage, spoilage, insurance, theft
and handling, and the source puts warehouse holding cost as high as 30 per cent
of the value held. Beyond the direct cost, the capital, labour and energy sunk
into stock cannot be used anywhere else. And stock that sits unsold long enough
becomes obsolete, which is a total loss rather than a delayed sale.

**LIFO and FIFO.** These are valuation methods, not stock-handling methods. At
the end of a period a business must value what it sold (to get cost of goods
sold, and so gross profit) and what remains. When purchase prices change during
the period, the assumed order of sale changes both numbers.

- **FIFO** assumes the first goods purchased were the first sold, so cost of
  goods sold carries the oldest costs and the closing stock carries the newest.
- **LIFO** assumes the last goods purchased were the first sold, so cost of
  goods sold carries the newest costs and the closing stock carries the oldest.

When input prices are rising, LIFO puts the higher recent costs into cost of
goods sold, which understates gross profit and undervalues closing stock. FIFO
does the reverse: understated costs, overstated profit, and closing stock
overvalued relative to what it would cost to replace. Both are permitted under
accounting standards, as is weighted average, which is why the method used must
be stated in the notes to the accounts. The consequence worth teaching is the
one the source states plainly: two businesses performing equally well can
legitimately report different profits purely from the valuation method chosen.

LIFO's claimed advantage is that recent costs more closely reflect current
economic value. Its cost is that in a period of falling prices it overstates
profit and so maximises tax.

**Just-in-time.** JIT aims to have inputs arrive only as the process needs them,
in the exact quantity needed, at the point they are needed. Make to order rather
than make to stock. It removes holding and insurance cost, minimises shrinkage
and obsolescence, and lets a retailer display a wider range because less of each
line is stored.

JIT is not free. It requires a responsive operations function with flexible
processing, the ability to respond quickly to demand changes, and supplier
deliveries that are reliable to the appointed time. Each of those is itself an
investment, and the last one is a dependency on a business the manager does not
control.

JIT sits inside lean production alongside **kaizen** (continuous incremental
improvement) and **jidoka** (automation with the authority for any worker to
stop the line when a defect appears, so the defect is corrected before it
propagates). Lean arose where demand was too low for economies of scale to pay,
which is the condition that makes it a strategy rather than a default.

## Relationships to other concepts

Inventory management is a **cause** acting on performance objectives, and it is
the clearest example in the syllabus of one strategy moving several objectives
in opposite directions at once.

| holding more stock | holding less stock (JIT) |
| --- | --- |
| raises **dependability**: demand is met when it appears | lowers dependability if a supplier is late, because there is no buffer |
| raises **speed** of delivery to the customer: lead time shortens | raises **flexibility**: a smaller committed range is easier to change |
| lowers **flexibility**: capital is committed to what was already made | lowers **cost**: no holding, insurance, shrinkage or obsolescence |
| raises **cost**: holding, insurance, obsolescence, and capital tied up | raises **cost** elsewhere: responsive processing and reliable supply have to be bought |

That table is the whole reason this strategy is worth a paragraph. A student who
writes "JIT reduces costs" has stated a third of it. A student who writes "JIT
reduces holding costs but transfers the risk of a supply failure to a supplier
the business does not control, which is a dependability cost the business accepts
because its market rewards range over certainty" has assessed it.

Inventory management also reaches directly into finance, which is unusual for an
operations strategy: the valuation method changes reported gross profit, and
stock is a current asset that affects liquidity. A student can legitimately use
inventory to link operations and financial management, which examiners reward.

## Terminology

- **inventory / stock** — raw materials, work in progress and finished goods
  held at a point in time
- **LIFO (last in first out)** — valuation assuming the most recently purchased
  goods are sold first
- **FIFO (first in first out)** — valuation assuming the earliest purchased
  goods are sold first
- **inventory valuation** — determining the value of stock sold and stock
  remaining at the end of a period
- **just in time (JIT)** — inputs arrive only as the process needs them
- **lean production** — a method emphasising the removal of waste
- **kaizen** — continuous improvement by regular small changes
- **jidoka** — automation with a human check; a worker can halt production on
  seeing a defect
- **cellular manufacturing** — grouping people and machines into cells producing
  similar items or using a similar process
- **obsolescence** — loss of value from stock remaining unsold too long
- **shrinkage** — loss of stock to theft, damage or error
- **lead time** — the interval between an order and its delivery

## Distinctions and confusions

- **JIT is not an alternative to LIFO or FIFO.** JIT manages the flow and
  storage of stock; LIFO and FIFO value it. A business can run JIT and still use
  either valuation method. The source makes this point explicitly because
  students get it wrong constantly.
- **FIFO is not "selling the oldest stock first".** It is an assumption used for
  costing. The physical order goods leave the warehouse in need not match it,
  though for perishables it usually does.
- **JIT does not eliminate inventory.** It minimises it and moves the buffer up
  the supply chain. Someone is still holding stock; it is the supplier.
- **Lean is not the same as cheap.** Lean removes waste. It requires investment
  in flexibility, supplier relationships and information systems that a
  high-stock business does not need.
- **Holding stock is not bad management.** It is a purchased insurance against
  lost sales. Whether it is worth its cost depends on how much a lost sale costs
  this particular business.

## Worked example, structurally

1. name the inventory approach the business uses
2. name the performance objective it is chosen to serve
3. state the mechanism: what about the approach actually moves that objective
4. name the objective it costs, and why the business accepts that
5. say what the business's market makes that trade worth taking

Step 4 is available here more easily than for any other operations strategy,
because the trade-offs are structural rather than incidental. A paragraph on
inventory that names only a benefit has left the easiest marks on the table.

## Adjacent concepts

Performance objectives (especially cost, dependability, flexibility), supply
chain management and supplier reliability, quality management (jidoka is a
quality practice inside a lean system), technology (inventory control software
is what makes JIT executable), gross profit and cost of goods sold, liquidity.

## What the source does not give us

- The advantages and disadvantages of holding stock are given as two lists and
  never resolved into a trade-off. Nothing says which business should choose
  which, or on what basis.
- No link is drawn from inventory choices to named performance objectives, even
  though this chapter defines those objectives five sections earlier.
- The LIFO and FIFO worked example is arithmetic. It never asks what a manager
  would do differently knowing the difference, which is the only reason a
  Business Studies student needs it.
- The 30 per cent holding cost figure is unsourced and cannot be used as student
  evidence as it stands.
