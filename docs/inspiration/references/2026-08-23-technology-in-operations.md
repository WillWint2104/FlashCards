## Definition

**Leading edge technology** is the most advanced or innovative technology
available at a point in time.

**Established technology** is technology already developed, widely used and
accepted without question.

The syllabus treats these as two ends of one strategy, and the decision between
them is the strategy. A business is not choosing whether to use technology; it is
choosing how far ahead of the field to be.

Technology in operations is classified two ways: by whether it acts on inputs,
transformation processes or outputs, or by whether it makes managerial and
administrative work run more smoothly.

## Explanation

**Leading edge.** A business adopting the best available technology can
differentiate its operations process: produce faster, hit higher standards,
waste less, and operate more effectively. Some businesses go further and create
the technology themselves, which is where operations meets research and
development.

The important structural point is that leading edge technology is created by
processes and thinking, and once created it can change the market itself rather
than only the business using it. New inputs make new products possible. That is
a different kind of consequence from a productivity gain, and it is the reason
"leading edge" is a strategy rather than a purchase.

**Bleeding edge** is the term for technology so new that adopting it carries a
real risk of unreliability. The source gives it in a single aside, and it is the
most useful word in the section, because it names the cost of leading edge
adoption that the rest of the section leaves out.

**Established.** Widely used, functionally sound, and it sets basic standards of
productivity and speed. In operations that means:

- barcoding and point-of-sale data for inventory management
- robotics for complex and detailed manufacturing
- CAD, CAM and CIM for designing and integrating transformation processes
- information processing and IT for administration, logistics, input modelling,
  demand analysis and distribution
- flexible manufacturing systems for transformation processes

Note what these do: established technology is what makes the other operations
strategies executable. JIT is not possible without inventory control systems.
E-procurement is not possible without the IT link. Six Sigma needs statistical
measurement of a process. Established technology is infrastructure, not
advantage.

## Relationships to other concepts

Technology is a **cause** acting on performance objectives, and it is the only
operations strategy that acts on almost all of them at once, which is exactly
why it is dangerous to write about. An answer that says technology improves
speed, quality, cost and flexibility has said something true and worthless. The
mark is in the choice between leading edge and established, and that choice has a
clean trade-off:

| leading edge | established |
| --- | --- |
| buys **differentiation**: the business can do what competitors cannot yet do | buys **dependability**: it is proven, supported, and staff can be hired who already know it |
| high cost, paid before any return is known | lower cost, and often falling, since the price of a mature technology declines |
| high risk: bleeding edge technology may not work reliably | low risk, but no advantage, because competitors have it too |
| the advantage is temporary, since it becomes established the moment it spreads | the advantage was never there; it is the price of competing |

The last row is the sentence to build a paragraph on. **Leading edge technology
converts into established technology by being successful.** A business pursuing
differentiation through technology is committing to keep buying, because the
advantage decays on a schedule set by competitors' adoption.

Technology also sits directly behind cost leadership and differentiation as the
strategic role of operations: established technology serves cost leadership,
leading edge serves differentiation. That link makes technology the easiest
strategy to connect back to the business's overall strategic position.

The bill for technology falls due in human resources, which is where it connects
to overcoming resistance to change: new equipment requires retraining, may make
existing skills redundant, and often forces a plant layout to be rebuilt.

## Terminology

- **leading edge technology** — the most advanced or innovative at a point in
  time; also called cutting edge
- **bleeding edge technology** — so new that unreliability is a material risk to
  the adopter
- **established technology** — developed, widely used, accepted without question
- **CAD (computer-aided design)** — designing the product digitally
- **CAM (computer-aided manufacturing)** — controlling the making of it
- **CIM (computer-integrated manufacturing)** — linking design and manufacture
  into one system
- **FMS (flexible manufacturing system)** — a system able to switch between
  products without lengthy retooling
- **POS (point of sale) data** — sales data captured at the till, used to drive
  inventory decisions
- **robotics** — automated machinery performing complex or detailed manufacture
- **capital cost** — the up-front cost of acquiring equipment, recouped through
  use and depreciation

## Distinctions and confusions

- **Leading edge is not the same as good.** It is the newest, not the most
  suitable. Bleeding edge technology is by definition leading edge and may be
  the wrong choice for a business that cannot absorb the risk of it failing.
- **Established is not the same as obsolete.** Established technology is current
  and functional. It simply confers no advantage, because everyone has it.
- **Technology is not automatically a productivity gain.** The benefit cannot be
  realised without spending on training and development, and the source says so.
  An answer that stops at the purchase has stopped one step early.
- **Flexible manufacturing systems are established, not leading edge.** Students
  classify anything with an acronym as advanced. The test is whether it is
  widely used and accepted, not whether it sounds new.
- **The classification is time-dependent.** Every established technology was
  leading edge once. "Is this leading edge?" is a question about a date, not
  about a device.

## Worked example, structurally

1. name the technology and classify it, leading edge or established, and say why
   the classification holds at this date
2. name the performance objective it serves
3. state the mechanism concretely: what step in the transformation process
   changed
4. name the cost, and prefer the non-obvious ones: the training, the downtime,
   the risk of unreliability, the fact that the advantage decays
5. judge whether the position was worth holding, given who this business
   competes against

Step 4 is what turns an answer about technology from a list of benefits into an
argument. Step 1 also carries marks, because most students never justify the
classification and it is the only classification in the syllabus that changes
over time.

## Adjacent concepts

Performance objectives, all six; cost leadership and differentiation as the
strategic role; research and development; overcoming resistance to change,
including retraining and plant layout; inventory management and quality
management, both of which depend on established technology to run; e-commerce
and e-procurement; technology as an influence on operations.

## What the source does not give us

- No trade-off between leading edge and established. Both sections describe
  benefits and neither describes a cost, so there is nothing to choose between.
- The word that names the risk, bleeding edge, appears once as an aside and is
  never used again.
- No account of advantage decaying, even though the definition of established
  technology implies it directly.
- No link to any named performance objective, and no link to cost leadership or
  differentiation, though both are in the same course.
- Company examples are illustrations of adoption, not evidence about outcomes,
  and none carries a figure a student could deploy.
