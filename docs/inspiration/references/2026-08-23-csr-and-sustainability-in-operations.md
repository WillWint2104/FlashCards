## Concepts (SOURCE)

**Corporate social responsibility (CSR)** is open and accountable business
action based on respect for people, community and the broader environment. Its
defining feature is doing more than complying with law and regulation.

**Environmental sustainability** (ecological sustainability) means shaping
operations around practices that consume resources today without compromising
access to those resources for future generations.

**Triple bottom line** is the earlier name for the same idea: financial
profitability, social impact and environmental impact, weighed together. CSR
means the driver of decision making is not profitability alone.

## Explanation (SOURCE)

**Environmental sustainability has three parts**, and naming all three is worth
marks because students usually give one:

1. sustainable use of renewable resources
2. reduced use of non-renewable resources
3. the **precautionary principle** — where the environmental impact is
   uncertain, take the action most likely to cause the least harm

The third is the interesting one, because it is a decision rule for acting under
uncertainty. It says the absence of proof of harm is not a reason to proceed.

In operations this shows up as waste minimisation, recycling of water, glass,
paper and metals, and reducing the **carbon footprint**, the amount of carbon
entering the environment from the operations process. Businesses formalise it
with an **environmental management system**, which can be accredited to ISO
14001, an international standard that gives stakeholders confidence the business
is measuring and improving its impact. That parallel with ISO 9001 in quality is
worth pointing out: in both cases certification proves a system exists, not that
its outcomes are good.

**Why CSR is specifically an operations concern.** The syllabus asks students to
explain this, and the answer has three parts, each a different point in the
transformation process:

- **inputs** — the business must know where and how its inputs are sourced, and
  buy only from suppliers meeting appropriate standards
- **processes** — processes must be shaped to minimise environmental damage and
  waste
- **people** — recruitment must draw from a diverse range of employees and be
  inclusive

Operations is where CSR either happens or does not, because operations is where
the resources are consumed and the labour is used. A CSR policy that is not
expressed in the transformation process is a statement, not a practice.

**Legal compliance against ethical responsibility.** Legal compliance means
following the letter of the law, the prescribed standard of behaviour. Ethical
responsibility means meeting every legal obligation and then following the
intention and spirit of the law as well. A business acting ethically is signalling
that it values something beyond maximum profit.

Compliance is broad, and the range is worth having available: labour law
(minimum and award wages, hours, breaks, leave, workers compensation, work health
and safety); environmental and public health regulation (dumping, pollution of
air, land and water, waste disposal standards); business licensing (training and
certification requirements, restricted hours, zoning, disclosure); taxation
including superannuation, which can be structured to encourage or penalise
particular behaviour; trade practices (market power, misleading conduct, price
collusion, market concentration, product safety); migration rules on offshore
skilled labour; intellectual property (copyright, patents, trademarks, designs);
financial and accounting regulation and corporations law, including directors'
duties as **fiduciaries**; **corporate governance**, the rules and policies
directing the business, which places responsibility on the board for integrity in
decisions affecting stakeholders; and human rights, including anti-discrimination
on disability, culture, sexual preference, gender and age.

**The uncomfortable part, and the best material in the section.** Compliance
costs money. Since the main goal of a business is profit, many choose the lowest
level of compliance the law permits, because that is the lowest cost.

Offshore outsourcing then becomes a compliance strategy rather than only an
operations one. It takes advantage of regulatory difference: lower taxes, lower
labour standards, weaker environmental and intellectual property rules. The
saving is real and its source is the weaker rule.

And the source names the mechanism plainly: **using a third party can be a
screen behind which businesses hide.** A breach in another country becomes the
contractor's breach, with no legal responsibility falling on the business that
outsourced the production. Businesses have been exposed for being complicit by
not calling out contractors who did not follow local labour law.

That is the sharpest CSR argument available in the whole course. Legal
responsibility can be contracted away. Ethical responsibility cannot, and neither
can reputational responsibility, because customers attribute the harm to the
brand.

**Ethics across borders.** When laws differ between nations, "obey the law" stops
being a usable ethical rule, since the question becomes *which* law. Businesses
resolve it by consulting special interest groups or adopting the standards of
international bodies. The **International Labour Organization** is the example:
a tripartite United Nations agency bringing together governments, employers and
worker representatives to set labour standards. Its conference produces reports,
and where two thirds of member nations agree, a report's recommendations become
an **International Labour Standard** that nations are expected to legislate for.

**The business case.** The source states it clearly and it is the answer to "is
CSR worth it": socially responsible behaviour costs money in the short term and
often turns out to be in the business's interest in the long run. Customers
eventually identify who is acting responsibly. They stop buying from a business
they learn is exploiting employees, taking bribes or polluting, whether directly
or through a contractor. They reward those that behave well. A business can even
convert the position into a marketing strength.

Note the two conditions hidden in that argument: it depends on customers
*finding out*, and it pays *over the long run*. Both are places the argument can
fail, and naming them is what turns a description into an evaluation.

## Relationships and mechanisms (DERIVED unless marked)

CSR is an **influence** on operations, and ops-03 asks the reverse: how
operations strategies affect CSR. That direction is unusual and needs stating,
because it means the strategies are the cause and CSR outcomes are the effect.

Running each strategy in that direction:

| strategy | how it affects CSR |
| --- | --- |
| global sourcing and outsourcing | can lower standards by design, since the saving comes from weaker regulation; also the point where responsibility is most easily disclaimed and reputationally hardest to escape |
| supply chain management | long transport chains raise the carbon footprint; supplier auditing is how input standards are actually enforced |
| inventory management | JIT reduces obsolescence and waste, which is a genuine sustainability gain; more frequent smaller deliveries can raise transport emissions |
| quality management | right first time removes scrap and rework, which is waste reduction with a cost saving attached |
| technology | can reduce energy and waste per unit; also the source of the redundancies that make change socially costly |
| overcoming resistance to change | redundancy is the human cost of operations change, and how it is handled is a CSR question |
| product and service design | designing for recyclability, durability and low-impact inputs decides most of the environmental outcome before production starts |

The last row is worth emphasising. Most of a product's environmental impact is
committed at design, so design is where CSR is cheapest to achieve and hardest to
retrofit.

CSR also interacts with performance objectives in both directions: waste
reduction lowers **cost**, so some CSR is free; but higher input standards, fair
trade sourcing and emissions abatement raise cost, so some is not. An answer that
claims CSR always pays or always costs is wrong in the same way both times.

## Terminology (SOURCE)

- **corporate social responsibility (CSR)** — open and accountable action based
  on respect for people, community and environment, going beyond compliance
- **triple bottom line** — financial, social and environmental performance
  weighed together
- **environmental sustainability / ecological sustainability** — consuming
  resources today without compromising future access
- **precautionary principle** — where impact is uncertain, act to cause the least
  likely harm
- **carbon footprint** — carbon entering the environment from operations
  processes
- **environmental management system (EMS)** — a systematic approach to managing
  environmental impact; ISO 14001 is its international standard
- **legal compliance** — following the letter of the law
- **ethical responsibility** — meeting legal obligations and following the spirit
  of the law as well
- **compliance cost** — the cost of meeting regulatory requirements
- **corporate governance** — the rules, policies and decisions directing the
  business, and the board's responsibility for integrity
- **fiduciary** — a person in a position of financial trust over others' money
- **onshore / offshore outsourcing** — using a domestic provider, or one in
  another country
- **International Labour Organization (ILO)** — the tripartite UN agency setting
  labour standards
- **International Labour Standard** — a recommendation adopted by two thirds of
  ILO member nations, which nations are expected to legislate for
- **sustainability report** — a separate report on a business's ethical and
  environmental commitments, beyond the annual report

## Distinctions and confusions

- **CSR is not compliance.** Compliance is the floor. CSR is what a business
  does above it. A business that only obeys the law is not demonstrating CSR, it
  is avoiding penalties.
- **Legal responsibility can be outsourced; ethical responsibility cannot.**
  This is the most useful sentence in the topic and the source states the
  mechanism for it explicitly.
- **Ethical is not the same as legal, in either direction.** An action can be
  legal in one country and unethical by the standards of another, which is
  exactly the situation offshore outsourcing creates.
- **Sustainability is not only environmental.** The triple bottom line has three
  parts. Students answer sustainability questions with recycling and never reach
  the social one.
- **The precautionary principle is not a ban.** It is a rule for acting when
  impact is uncertain, and it selects the least harmful action rather than
  forbidding action.
- **Certification is not performance.** ISO 14001 certifies that a system for
  measuring and improving impact exists, in the same way ISO 9001 does for
  quality.
- **CSR is not free and it is not always costly.** Waste reduction saves money;
  higher input standards do not. Which one applies depends on the specific
  practice.

## Instructional implications (USE)

1. name the operations decision, precisely: a sourcing standard, a process
   change, a design choice, a technology
2. name which part of CSR it acts on: environmental, social, or governance
3. state the mechanism: what physically changes in the transformation process
4. name what it costs, or what it saves, and be honest about which
5. judge whether the business went beyond compliance or only met it, and say how
   you can tell

Step 5 is the assessment, and the test in it is the useful one: could the
business have done less and still been legal? If yes, that is CSR. If no, it is
compliance.

## Adjacent concepts

Every operations strategy, since ops-03 asks how each affects CSR; globalisation
and regulatory difference; outsourcing and offshoring; supply chain management
and supplier auditing; quality management and waste; government policy and legal
regulation as influences; stakeholder expectations; ethical business behaviour in
marketing, finance and human resources.

## Source gaps

- Nothing running in the ops-03 direction. Every treatment here is CSR as an
  influence on operations. Nothing asks how an operations strategy changes CSR
  outcomes, which is what the question wants.
- No cost account. CSR is said to cost money short term and pay long term, with
  no worked case of either, and no discussion of a business for which it did not
  pay.
- The two conditions the business case rests on, that customers find out and
  that the horizon is long, are never named as conditions.
- Case material is company-reported: targets, principles, workplace rankings and
  emissions reductions all sourced from the businesses themselves or from awards.
  Background for authoring, not student evidence.
