## Concepts (SOURCE)

The four Ps were written for goods. As services grew into most of the economy,
three more were added for the parts of a purchase a customer experiences rather
than owns: **people**, **processes** and **physical evidence**. The seven
together are the extended marketing mix.

- **people** — the quality of the interaction between the customer and whoever
  in the business delivers the service.
- **processes** — the flow of activities a business follows in delivering the
  service: how the customer finds the product, selects it, pays for it, receives
  it, and gets help afterwards.
- **physical evidence** — everything the customer sees while interacting with
  the business: premises, fit-out, signage, uniforms, brochures, the website,
  and the visible reviews of other customers.

## Explanation (SOURCE)

The reason these three exist as separate elements is that a service cannot be
inspected before purchase. A customer buying a jacket can hold it. A customer
booking a courier, a haircut or a flight is buying a promise, and has to judge
that promise from proxies. The three extra Ps are exactly those proxies.

**People.** Frontline staff are the marketing message being delivered in
person. How they greet, how they handle an enquiry, how they handle a complaint
is not customer service running alongside marketing, it is the product being
consumed. This is why the element reaches back into human resource management:
the marketing decision to promise attentive service is only kept by recruitment,
training and a culture the leadership actually maintains.

**Processes.** Behind a service that feels fast sits a designed sequence.
Ordering a flight online is a chain of steps — enter details, receive a
reference, select a seat, download a boarding pass — and each step is a place
the promise can be kept or broken. The test the source offers is a good one for
students: are the systems designed for the customer's benefit, or for the
convenience of the business and its staff? Payment options, queue length,
delivery windows, invoice accuracy and how fast messages are returned are all
process decisions, and each is visible to a customer who never thinks of them as
"processes".

**Physical evidence.** The restaurant example carries the idea: product, price,
people and process can all be right and the dirty cutlery still loses the
customer. Because there is no sample to try, the customer buys on trust and
builds that trust from what is visible. That now includes the digital premises.
A site that is easy to navigate, with clear contact details and a secure
checkout, is physical evidence in the same way a clean shopfront is. So are
independent reviews, which is why encouraging satisfied customers to review is a
physical evidence strategy rather than a promotion one.

These three connect marketing to operations and human resources more tightly
than the original four, which is the interdependence point examiners like.

## Relationships and mechanisms (DERIVED unless marked)

This is the part the source states least and `mkt-01` needs most. The question
is *how target markets affect* these strategies, so every note here has to run
in that direction: **a characteristic of the target market causes the strategy
to take the form it takes.**

The source describes each element and leaves the causal step to the reader. The
step looks like this:

| target market characteristic | what it forces |
| --- | --- |
| customers buying on trust with no way to sample | physical evidence carries the whole quality signal, so premises and reviews are the strategy |
| customers who value expertise over speed | people: hire for knowledge, train for consultation, allow longer interactions |
| customers who value speed over expertise | processes: standardise the sequence, remove decision points, invest in the delivery system |
| customers who expect to self-serve | processes moves online, and physical evidence shifts from premises to interface |
| a market segment defined by price | processes are designed to strip cost, and physical evidence must reassure without expense |
| a segment defined by luxury positioning | physical evidence is deliberately expensive because the expense is the message |

The three also constrain each other:

- **people against processes.** The more the process is systemised, the less
  discretion frontline staff have. A franchise gets consistency by removing
  individual judgement; a consultancy gets value by preserving it. A business
  cannot maximise both, and which one it picks is set by the target market.
- **physical evidence against cost.** Premises, fit-out and staffed service
  centres are held capital. A business keeps them when its target market values
  being able to walk in and speak to someone, and drops them when the market has
  moved online.
- **processes and physical evidence reinforce online.** When the process is
  digital, the interface *is* the physical evidence. The same design decision
  serves both, which is the one pair students can safely say pulls together.

## Terminology (SOURCE)

- **extended marketing mix** — the original four Ps plus people, processes and
  physical evidence
- **people** — the quality of interaction between customer and service deliverer
- **processes** — the flow of activities in delivering a service
- **physical evidence** — everything the customer sees when interacting with the
  business, physical or digital
- **intangible product** — a service, which cannot be inspected before purchase
- **systemisation** — designing the sequence so it can be repeated identically
  across sites or people; the mechanism behind franchise consistency
- **points of presence** — the physical locations a business keeps even when the
  transaction could happen online
- **servicescape** — the designed environment a service is delivered in. Not the
  source's word, but the one that names the idea precisely enough to teach with.

## Distinctions and confusions

- **People is not human resource management.** HRM is how staff are recruited,
  trained and paid. People as a marketing strategy is the customer-facing
  outcome of that. A student who writes a paragraph about training programs and
  never reaches the customer has answered a different question.
- **Processes is not operations.** Operations is the transformation of inputs
  into outputs. Processes in the marketing mix is only the part the customer
  moves through. The overlap is real but the marketing question is always "what
  did the customer experience".
- **Physical evidence is not place.** Place is the channel and how the product
  reaches the customer. Physical evidence is what the customer sees at the point
  of contact. The same store is place when you ask *how it gets there* and
  physical evidence when you ask *what it signals*.
- **Physical evidence is not only physical.** The website, the confirmation
  email and the review page are all physical evidence. Students routinely limit
  it to the shopfront and lose half the marks available.
- **These are not services-only.** Goods carry a service component around them,
  such as after-sales support, so the three Ps apply to a retailer too.

## Instructional implications (USE)

For an "explain how target markets affect ..." question, the paragraph shape
that earns the marks:

1. name the target market by a characteristic, not a demographic label alone
2. name the element and the specific form it takes in this business
3. state the causal step: because the market is like that, the element had to
   take that form
4. give the case detail that shows it, and say what the detail demonstrates
5. name what would have been chosen instead for a different market

Step 3 is the one students skip. A paragraph that describes the strategy and
mentions a target market has described two things side by side. Step 5 is what
proves the link was causal rather than coincidental.

  described on its own terms and the connective work is left undone. That
