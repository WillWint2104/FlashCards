## Definition

Global financial management is managing the additional financial risks a
business takes on when it transacts across borders. The syllabus names five:

- **exchange rates**
- **interest rates**
- **methods of international payment**
- **hedging**
- **derivatives**

The framing sentence worth keeping is the source's own: the financial risks of
global expansion are greater than domestic ones, and taking them is necessary for
the strategy to be implemented. Risk here is not a mistake, it is a price.

## Explanation

**Exchange rates.** Every country uses its own currency domestically, so a
cross-border transaction requires one currency to be converted into another. The
**foreign exchange market** is where dealers buy and sell each other's
currencies, and their trading is what establishes the price of one currency in
terms of another: the **exchange rate**.

Rates move with demand and supply, and the movement has two names and two
consequences:

- **Appreciation** — the domestic currency rises. Each unit of foreign currency
  now buys fewer domestic dollars, and each domestic dollar buys more foreign
  currency. Exports become more expensive abroad, imports become cheaper at
  home. Net effect: the international competitiveness of exporters falls.
- **Depreciation** — the opposite. Exports become cheaper abroad, imports more
  expensive at home, and exporters become more competitive.

The rule students should be able to state without hesitating: **a rising dollar
helps importers and hurts exporters; a falling dollar does the reverse.**

Because revenue and expenses move between countries, exchange rate movement
changes their value, and so directly changes profitability and the business's
ability to meet its financial objectives. This is the clearest example in the
course of an objective being affected by something entirely outside the
business's control.

**Interest rates.** A business expanding offshore or building capacity for
export usually needs to raise finance, and it can borrow at home or overseas.
Where domestic rates are higher than those abroad, borrowing overseas looks
cheaper.

The trap is the point of the section: **the saving is denominated in a currency
that moves.** An adverse currency movement can eliminate the interest advantage
entirely, so cheap money borrowed abroad can end up costing more. A student who
identifies that the interest rate risk and the exchange rate risk are the same
risk wearing two hats has understood the topic.

**Methods of international payment.** The underlying problem is old and simple:
neither party trusts the other. If the exporter ships before payment, there is no
guarantee of being paid. If the importer pays before shipment, there is no
guarantee of receiving the goods. The solution is a third party both trust,
normally a bank acting as intermediary.

Four methods, in order of increasing risk to the exporter:

| method | how it works | exporter's risk |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **payment in advance** | the exporter is paid, then sends the goods | least; used for subsidiaries or where the buyer's creditworthiness is uncertain, but few importers accept it because it puts all the risk on them |
| **letter of credit** | the importer's bank promises to pay a specified amount once conditions are met and shipping documents are presented; the commitment is essentially irrevocable and the bank must pay if the buyer cannot | low, and popular, because the exporter is relying on a bank rather than on the importer |
| **bill of exchange** | a document drawn by the exporter demanding payment at a specified time; the exporter keeps control of the goods until payment is made or guaranteed | moderate, and it has two forms |
| **clean payment** | the exporter ships directly to the importer before payment is received | most |

The two bill forms differ in one respect that decides everything: with
**document against payment**, the importer's bank releases the documents needed
to collect the goods only after payment. With **document against acceptance**,
the importer signs acceptance of the goods and the terms, receives the documents,
collects the goods, and pays later. The first keeps the goods as security; the
second extends credit.

Which method is chosen depends on the exporter's assessment of the importer's
creditworthiness. That is the sentence that makes this a decision rather than a
list.

**Hedging.** A **spot exchange** is an immediate exchange at the day's **spot
rate**. Since rates move constantly, a business exposed to them faces higher
costs and lower profit at unpredictable moments. **Hedging** is minimising that
risk to reduce the uncertainty in international transactions.

*Natural hedging* means arranging the business so exposure cancels out, without
buying any financial product:

- establishing offshore subsidiaries, so costs are incurred in the same currency
  as revenue
- arranging import payments and export receipts in the same foreign currency, so
  a loss on one is offset by a gain on the other
- marketing strategies that reduce the price sensitivity of exported products, so
  a rate movement costs less volume
- insisting both import and export contracts are denominated in the home
  currency, which does not remove the risk but transfers it to the other party

That last one is worth flagging as an ethics-adjacent point: the risk has not
disappeared, it has moved to whoever had less bargaining power.

*Financial instrument hedging* uses **derivatives**, financial products designed
to reduce or spread exchange rate risk. Three matter:

- **forward exchange contract** — an agreement to exchange one currency for
  another at an agreed rate on a future date, typically 30, 90 or 180 days out.
  The bank guarantees the exporter a fixed rate for the proceeds of the sale, so
  the value of an invoice is locked in.
- **option** — the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell currency at a
  future time. The holder is protected from unfavourable movement and keeps the
  benefit of favourable movement. That asymmetry is what makes an option
  different from a forward, and it is not free.
- **currency swap** — exchanging currency in the spot market with an agreement
  to reverse the transaction later. Also used to raise finance in a currency
  where the business is not well known and would pay a premium: two businesses
  each borrow where they are known and cheap, then swap and repay each other's
  loan. Its main advantage is altering exposure without discarding the original
  transaction.

The analogy the source offers is a good one to reuse: hedging is like fixing the
interest rate on a loan. You gain certainty and the ability to plan, and you give
up the benefit of a favourable move. And the warning attached is the one students
never include: **derivatives used unwisely can be as dangerous as the risks they
are meant to protect against.**

## Relationships to other concepts

Global financial management is a **cause** acting on financial objectives, and
it is different in kind from the other three strategies. Cash flow, working
capital and profitability management all improve a position. This one **protects
one from something outside the business's control.**

| decision | objective protected | objective paid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| hedging with a forward contract | profitability, by fixing a known value | the upside of a favourable movement, which is a real cost even though it never appears as one |
| hedging with an option | profitability, keeping the upside | the premium paid for the option |
| natural hedge via offshore subsidiary | profitability, by matching currencies | flexibility and capital, since the subsidiary is a commitment |
| letter of credit | liquidity and profitability, by removing non-payment risk | bank charges, and the importer's willingness to trade on those terms |
| borrowing overseas at lower interest | profitability, through lower interest cost | exposure to currency movement, which can exceed the saving |

The idea to build a paragraph on: **hedging does not make a business more
profitable, it makes it more predictable.** A business that hedges every exposure
gives up the gains as well as the losses. Whether that is worth it depends on
whether the business can survive the bad outcome, not on which outcome is more
likely. That is a genuine judgement, and it is available for an "assess" or
"evaluate" command word.

Payment method choice is a **liquidity** decision as much as a risk decision:
payment in advance brings cash in before the goods leave, while clean payment
means the cash arrives long after the cost was incurred. The working capital
cycle of an exporter is set by the payment method it can persuade its customers
to accept.

The topic also connects to marketing (price sensitivity of exports is a
marketing property being used as a financial hedge), to operations (offshore
subsidiaries and global sourcing put costs in the currency where revenue is
earned), and to fin-03, since exchange rates and interest rates are the two
external influences on financial management that a business can only respond to.

## Terminology

- **foreign exchange market (forex)** — where currencies are traded and their
  relative prices set
- **exchange rate** — the ratio of one currency to another
- **appreciation** — an upward movement of a currency against another
- **depreciation** — a downward movement
- **spot exchange / spot rate** — an immediate exchange at the day's rate
- **hedging** — minimising the risk of currency fluctuation
- **natural hedge** — reducing exposure by how the business is arranged rather
  than by a financial product
- **derivative** — a financial instrument used to reduce or spread currency risk
- **forward exchange contract** — a fixed rate agreed now for an exchange on a
  future date
- **option** — the right but not the obligation to buy or sell currency later
- **currency swap** — a spot exchange with an agreed future reversal
- **payment in advance** — payment received before the goods are sent
- **letter of credit** — a bank's guarantee of payment once documented conditions
  are met
- **bill of exchange** — a document demanding payment at a specified time, in
  either document-against-payment or document-against-acceptance form
- **clean payment** — goods shipped before payment is received
- **creditworthiness** — the assessed likelihood that a buyer will pay

## Distinctions and confusions

- **Appreciation is good for importers and bad for exporters.** Students reverse
  this constantly. Tie it to a sentence they can rebuild: a strong dollar buys
  more overseas, so imports are cheap; and foreigners must pay more for our
  goods, so exports are dear.
- **Hedging is not speculation.** Hedging removes exposure. Trading currency to
  profit from movement adds it. They use the same instruments in opposite
  directions.
- **Hedging is not free and it is not always right.** The cost is the forgone
  favourable movement, or the option premium. A business that can absorb the bad
  outcome may rationally choose not to hedge.
- **An option is not a forward.** A forward is an obligation. An option is a
  right, which is why it costs a premium and keeps the upside.
- **A natural hedge does not always remove risk.** Denominating contracts in your
  own currency transfers the risk to the other party rather than removing it.
- **Cheaper overseas interest is not cheaper borrowing.** The exchange rate can
  more than cancel the rate difference.
- **The payment methods differ in who holds the risk, not in how much it costs.**
  Ordering them by exporter risk is the point, and knowing that clean payment is
  riskiest and payment in advance safest is worth marks on its own.

## Worked example, structurally

1. name the exposure precisely: which currency, on which side of the transaction,
   over what period
2. name the strategy chosen, and be specific about the instrument or arrangement
3. state the mechanism: what is fixed, matched or transferred, and by when
4. name what it cost: the premium, the forgone upside, the commitment, or the
   party the risk was moved to
5. judge it against the business's capacity to bear the unhedged outcome, not
   against what the rate did afterwards

Step 5 is the discipline that makes a hedging answer sound like judgement rather
than hindsight. A hedge that turned out unnecessary was not a bad decision.

## What the source does not give us

- No cost of hedging is stated other than the loan analogy. The forgone upside is
  never named as a cost, so hedging reads as free protection.
- No basis for choosing between the three derivatives, and no condition under
  which a business should not hedge at all.
- The four payment methods are ranked by risk and never connected to liquidity or
  the working capital cycle, though the timing difference between payment in
  advance and clean payment is enormous.
- No link from any of it to the named financial objectives.
- The figures given (a manufacturer's currency losses over five years, cash rate
  history) are company and central bank reporting quoted second hand. Background
  for authoring, not student evidence.
