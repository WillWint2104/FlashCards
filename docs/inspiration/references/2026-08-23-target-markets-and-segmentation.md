## Concepts (SOURCE)

A **target market** is the group of present and potential customers a business
intends to sell to. Its members share characteristics such as age, income,
lifestyle, location and spending patterns.

**Market segmentation** is the act of dividing the total market into groups that
share one or more characteristics. Segmentation produces the segments; selecting
one of them produces the target market. The two words are not interchangeable
and students use them as though they are.

A **segmentation variable** is the characteristic used to do the dividing.

## Explanation (SOURCE)

A business identifies a target market so it can point its marketing at a group
rather than at everybody. What that buys, in the source's terms:

- marketing resources are spent more efficiently, so campaigns cost less per
  customer reached
- promotional material is relevant enough to be noticed
- buying behaviour becomes knowable, because the group is uniform enough to have
  patterns
- data can be compared over time within one defined group
- the strategies themselves can be refined against a known audience

**Three approaches to selecting one.**

| approach | what the business does | when it fits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| mass marketing | one product, one price, one promotion, one distribution system, aimed at the whole market | demand is genuinely uniform, and scale is the source of advantage |
| market segmentation | divide the market, pick a segment, build a mix for it | needs differ enough that a single mix satisfies nobody well |
| niche | a segment within a segment, a narrow micro-market | large businesses find the group too small to be worth altering their mix for |

The niche approach works precisely because of what the source says about its
neglect: big businesses rarely find it profitable to alter their marketing mix
for very small groups, and that unprofitability is the small business's shelter
from competition.

**Primary and secondary target markets.** A business may run both. The primary
market receives most of the marketing resources and generates most of the
revenue, and its customers are the loyal repeat buyers. The 80/20 principle is
the usual shorthand: roughly 80 per cent of sales come from 20 per cent of
customers. The secondary market is smaller but is not surplus. It is the hedge
against losing the primary one.

**The four segmentation variables.**

| variable | divides by | examples of the variable |
| --- | --- | --- |
| demographic | measurable features of a population | age, gender, income, education, occupation, social class, religion, ethnicity, family size |
| geographic | where people are | region, urban / suburban / rural, city size, climate, landforms |
| psychographic | why people behave as they do | lifestyle, personality, motives, socioeconomic group, opinions and interests |
| behavioural | the customer's relationship to the product itself | purchase occasion, benefits sought, loyalty, usage rate, price sensitivity |

Demographic variables are the most used because they are the easiest to measure.
That is a reason for their popularity, not a reason for their usefulness, and
the distinction is worth making to students: the easiest variable to collect is
rarely the one that explains the purchase.

Behavioural segmentation is the one that most directly generates strategy,
because it names what the customer wants *from the product*. Splitting a market
into users and non-users, then users into heavy, moderate and light, tells a
business exactly which group a price promotion or a redesign is aimed at.
Benefits sought is the single most productive variable for essay work, because
it hands the student a causal step for free.

## Relationships and mechanisms (DERIVED unless marked)

Target market is the **cause** end of every marketing argument. Segmentation
variables, the marketing mix and the extended mix are all downstream of it. That
is the shape `mkt-01` asks for, and it means a note about a strategy is only
half a note until it names the market characteristic that produced it.

The causal chain, stated in the order a paragraph should follow it:

1. the business segments the market by some variable
2. it selects a segment as the target market
3. the segment has a characteristic that constrains what will satisfy it
4. each element of the mix takes the form that characteristic demands
5. the elements are consistent with each other because they answer to the same
   characteristic

Step 5 is what makes a four-element answer hold together rather than read as
four separate paragraphs. The same target market pulls e-marketing, people,
processes and physical evidence in one direction. A business targeting
price-sensitive, self-serving customers ends up with cheap measurable channels,
minimal staffed interaction, a stripped and standardised process, and physical
evidence that reassures without costing. A business targeting a luxury segment
inverts all four. Neither business chose those four things separately.

**Changing the target market changes everything downstream.** The source's
rebranding case makes this well: a business that moves from customers in their
thirties and forties to a younger segment has to change product design,
endorsement, collaboration partners and channel all at once, because they were
never independent choices. This is the strongest available answer to "why does
the target market affect the strategies" and it is stated in a case study rather
than in the explanation, so a student is unlikely to find it.

## Terminology (SOURCE)

- **target market** — the group of present and potential customers a business
  intends to sell to
- **primary target market** — the segment receiving most of the marketing
  resources
- **secondary target market** — a smaller, less resourced segment kept as an
  alternative
- **market segmentation** — subdividing the total market into groups sharing one
  or more characteristics
- **segmentation variable** — the characteristic used to do the subdividing
- **mass marketing approach** — one mix directed at the whole market
- **niche market** — a narrowly selected segment; a segment within a segment
- **80/20 principle** — the observation that a small proportion of customers
  generates most sales
- **benefits sought** — what a customer wants the product to do for them, used
  as a behavioural segmentation variable
- **price sensitivity** — how far demand from a group responds to price, used as
  a behavioural variable. Note it is behavioural here, not demographic: it is
  about the relationship to the product, not about income.

## Distinctions and confusions

- **Segmentation is not the target market.** Segmentation is the dividing;
  the target market is the segment chosen. Students write "the business used
  market segmentation" as though it were a strategy in itself.
- **A target market is not a demographic label.** "Young people" is not a target
  market for the purpose of an essay answer, because no strategy follows from
  it. "Customers who expect to buy without speaking to anyone" is, because four
  strategies follow from it immediately. The label that earns marks is the one
  that carries a consequence.
- **Niche is not the same as small.** A niche is defined by narrowness of need,
  not by number of customers. A niche can be lucrative and a mass market can be
  poor.
- **Primary is not the same as most numerous.** Primary means most resourced and
  most revenue-generating. The 80/20 principle means the primary market may well
  be the smaller group by headcount.
- **Demographic is not psychographic.** Age is demographic; the lifestyle
  someone of that age chooses is psychographic. Students name a psychographic
  variable and classify it as demographic constantly, because the case examples
  mix the two in one sentence.
- **Mass marketing is a choice, not an absence of one.** It assumes uniform
  needs and commits to scale. Treating it as what a business does before it
  learns about segmentation misreads it.

## Instructional implications (USE)

1. name the segmentation variable the business used and the segment it selected
2. state the characteristic of that segment that matters, in terms of what the
   customer expects or will not tolerate
3. name the element of the mix and the form it takes
4. state the causal step explicitly: because the segment is like that, the
   element had to take that form
5. give a case detail as evidence and say what it demonstrates
6. show the consistency: name a second element pulled the same way by the same
   characteristic

Steps 4 and 6 are what the marks are for. A student who reaches step 3 has
described a target market and a strategy in the same paragraph without
connecting them, which is the most common failure on this question.

## Source gaps

- The causal direction is never stated as a principle. It is visible only inside
  one rebranding case study, and only to a reader already looking for it.
- Nothing on how the four segmentation variables interact, or on choosing
  between them. They are catalogued as four boxes.
- No treatment of a target market changing over time except in that one case,
  and no treatment of a business serving two target markets with genuinely
  different mixes.
- The figures on teenage spending, pocket money, revenue growth and housing
  demand are dated and second-hand. Useful as background for authoring, not
  usable as student evidence until someone verifies the primary source.
