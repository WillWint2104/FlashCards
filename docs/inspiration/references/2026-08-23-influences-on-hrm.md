## Concepts (SOURCE)

Two groups of influence, and students need to keep them apart.

**Stakeholders** — six named: employers, employees, employer associations, trade
unions, governments and government organisations, and society.

**Other influences** — legal (the employment contract, work health and safety,
anti-discrimination and equal employment opportunity), economic, technological,
social, and ethical including corporate social responsibility.

## Explanation (SOURCE)

### Stakeholders

**Employers** are, for legal purposes, those who exercise control over employees,
are responsible for paying wages or salaries, and have the power to dismiss. They
handle HR issues daily, some spending over half their working day on them, and
legislation increasingly encourages them to negotiate agreements and resolve
disputes at the individual workplace. The chapter is unusually critical here:
management surveys name people management as the most serious failing of
Australian business leaders, cited far more often than any other, which is why
improving management training matters.

**Employees** are workers under an employer's control, which covers workplace
location, how work is performed and the extent of supervision. The chapter's
characterisation: employees are on average more highly educated than in the past,
bore quickly, and demand more challenging work, involvement in decision making
and autonomy. After extensive downsizing many no longer trust one business to
look after their needs, so **churning**, moving frequently between organisations,
is increasing, particularly in services. Around a third want more work-life
balance. Older workers, women and younger inexperienced people struggle to obtain
full-time work and have become the flexible casual workforce. Participation by
people aged 65 and over is projected to rise substantially, as is women's
participation.

**Employer associations** represent and assist employer groups, usually as
respondents to the awards covering their members' employees.

**Trade unions** are organisations formed by employees in an industry, trade or
occupation to represent them in improving wages and working conditions. The
chapter traces their development through a series of test cases establishing the
basic wage and comparative wage justice, then sick leave, long service leave, the
40-hour week, and equal pay for equal work.

**Governments** act in five roles, and naming the role is what makes an answer
precise:

| role | what it means |
| --- | --- |
| **legislator** | passing the laws that provide the legal framework, and creating the institutions for bargaining and dispute resolution |
| **employer** | employing close to a third of Australian workers, and acting as pacesetter by introducing practices such as maternity leave, flexitime and affirmative action later adopted privately |
| **economic manager** | pursuing non-inflationary stable growth, which can conflict with employment goals, for instance cutting public service size to reduce a deficit |
| **administrator** | implementing legislation through departments and agencies, publishing guidance and investigating breaches |
| **international representative** | representing Australia in foreign affairs, trade and international labour matters, including as a foundation member of the ILO |

**Society** exerts influence through expectations about work, traced by the
chapter to the Universal Declaration of Human Rights recognising the importance
of work and the need for fair and just conditions. Employment is one of the most
significant issues for any voter.

### Legal influences

**The employment contract** is a legally binding formal agreement between
employer and employee, and every employee has one. A written contract gives more
protection than a verbal one, because disputes turn on one person's word against
another where arrangements are unclear. Two forms: **indefinite duration**, the
most common, continuing until either party gives notice as set by law or the
contract; and **fixed term**, ending at a defined date or on completion of a
specified task.

**Independent contractors** are the important contrast. They are typically
self-employed with a *contract for services* between principal and contractor,
against the *contract of service* an employer has with an employee. Contractors
lack employee rights, control their own work, are responsible for their own tax,
and may work for several businesses. Using them lets a firm bypass many
requirements of an employment contract such as sick leave and maternity
provisions. Where the relationship is unclear, courts apply a **control test**:
if the business had continuous, dominant and detailed control over the
contractor, it may be acting as an employer. Regular salary or wage payments,
regular specific hours, and PAYG deduction are further evidence.

**Work health and safety.** Driven by community and worker awareness and rising
compensation costs. **Safe Work Australia** conducts research and develops
national standards, codes of practice and common approaches, endorsed by state
governments, but constitutional limits mean enacting and enforcing WHS law falls
to the states, so the work has been to **harmonise** them into a national system,
intended to raise productivity by cutting compliance costs and to improve
conditions.

Under the NSW Act, employers must provide a safe system of work; ensure plant and
substances are used, handled, stored and transported safely; give information,
training and supervision; maintain the site safely; and ensure goods they design,
make, supply, install or repair do not injure others. All employers must hold
workers compensation insurance or face imprisonment or substantial fines, with
premiums varying by industry, wages paid, claims costs and the dust diseases
levy. Employers must also protect non-employees on site. Employees must take
reasonable care for others' health and safety, cooperate and comply, and can be
fined for bullying, skylarking or interfering with machinery. Health and safety
committees must be established in workplaces with more than 20 employees if a
majority of employees request it or a regulator directs it. Inspectors may
inspect, collect information and issue improvement notices.

**Anti-discrimination.** Discrimination is a policy or practice that
disadvantages a person or group because of a personal characteristic irrelevant
to the performance of the work. It includes **harassment**, offending behaviour
or intimidation, and **vilification**, a public act that is discriminatory and
incites hatred. Legislation protects against **direct and indirect**
discrimination across recruitment, selection, training, promotion, remuneration,
termination and access to other benefits. Protected grounds include race, sex,
sexual preference, colour, age, physical or mental disability, religious faith,
political opinion, social origin, national extraction, marital status, carer
responsibilities, and pregnancy or potential pregnancy.

To prevent it, employers must comply with legislation and **audit all policies
and practices** to ensure they do not discriminate. Named instruments include the
Human Rights and Equal Opportunity Commission Act 1986 (Cwlth), the Affirmative
Action (Equal Employment Opportunity for Women) Act 1986 (Cwlth), the Sex
Discrimination Act 1984 (Cwlth) and the Anti-Discrimination Act 1977 (NSW).
Supporting agencies: the Australian Human Rights Commission, the Workplace Gender
Equality Agency and the Anti-Discrimination Board (NSW). The Fair Work Act adds
freedom of association and protection across a wider range of employment aspects,
including dismissal, damaging an employee's ability to do their job, changing a
job to the employee's disadvantage, treating one employee differently, refusing
to employ, and not offering all the normal terms and conditions.

### Economic, technological, social and ethical

**Economic.** Demand for labour is derived from demand for goods and services. In
growth or peak phases, labour shortages appear, employers compete by offering
higher wages, and unions use stronger bargaining power to seek increases, which
can push up costs and prices. In a trough or recession, demand falls, businesses
downsize and their capacity to grant increases is limited.

**Technological.** The major source of improvement in productivity,
communication and competition. It changes the nature of production and services,
creates new jobs and makes others redundant. Businesses re-engineer and
restructure as networks, often offshore, using **virtual teams** and video
technology, and telecommuting lets firms operate anywhere at any time and reach
employees who would otherwise be disadvantaged by distance or congestion. The
cost the chapter names: it increases the need for ongoing training and for
protocols to maintain work-life balance where society expects people to be always
on call.

**Social.** Two named shifts. **Labour fragmentation**, the drift from
traditional full-time work toward part-time, driven by growth in finance, retail,
hospitality and community services, with part-time share roughly doubling over
several decades and particularly common among women. And **casualisation**, with
the casual share rising over the same period. The chapter notes the asymmetry:
most part-time workers do not want additional hours and value the flexibility,
while many casual workers would prefer more hours and would like paid holiday and
sick leave.

**Ethics and corporate social responsibility.** HR issues handled without ethics,
legality or social responsibility lead to poor morale, low productivity, heavy
costs and industrial disputes. **Ethical business practices** are those that are
socially responsible, morally right, honourable and fair.

## Load-bearing derived claims (DERIVED)

```
SOURCE  Independent contractors have a contract for services rather than a contract
        of service, lack employee rights, and using them lets a firm bypass
        requirements such as sick leave and maternity provisions. Courts apply a
        control test where the relationship is unclear.
DERIVED The contractor arrangement is a legal influence that a business can partly
        choose. Every other legal influence sets conditions the business must meet;
        this one lets a business decide which body of law applies to a worker, and
        the control test exists because that choice can be made in name only.
USE     The strongest single point for hr-02, because it shows an influence being
        managed rather than merely obeyed, which is what "analyse how key influences
        affect HRM" is asking for.
```

```
SOURCE  Government is listed as legislator, employer, economic manager,
        administrator and international representative, and the chapter notes that
        its economic goals can conflict with its employment goals.
DERIVED Those five roles pull in different directions on the same business.
        Government as employer sets a standard that raises expectations across the
        private sector; government as economic manager may cut the public service and
        raise unemployment; government as legislator then constrains how the
        resulting redundancies are handled.
USE     Lets a student write about government as a contested influence rather than a
        single actor, which is the difference between Analyse and Describe.
```

```
SOURCE  Most part-time workers do not want additional hours and value the
        flexibility, while many casual workers would prefer more hours and would like
        paid holiday and sick leave.
DERIVED Part-time and casual are not one trend. Part-time growth reflects worker
        preference and casual growth reflects employer preference, so the same
        statistic, a fall in full-time employment, has two opposite causes.
USE     A precision correction that turns a workforce statistic into an argument
        about whose interest is being served.
```

```
SOURCE  Technology lets firms operate anywhere at any time and reach employees
        otherwise disadvantaged by distance, and increases the need for ongoing
        training and for protocols to maintain work-life balance where people are
        expected to be always on call.
DERIVED Technology is the only influence in the chapter whose benefits and costs fall
        on the same person. It widens access to work and erodes the boundary around
        it, so a business gains flexibility and acquires a duty of care it did not
        previously have.
USE     A genuinely two-sided influence, which is what a "to what extent" or
        "analyse" question rewards.
```

```
SOURCE  Employers must comply with anti-discrimination legislation and audit all
        policies and practices to ensure they do not discriminate.
DERIVED The audit obligation makes indirect discrimination the harder problem.
        Direct discrimination is a decision someone made; indirect discrimination is
        a neutral policy with an unequal effect, and it can only be found by looking
        for it, which is what the audit is for.
USE     Explains why compliance here is an ongoing process rather than a rule to
        follow, and gives a student something concrete to recommend.
```

## Relationships and mechanisms (DERIVED unless marked)

Influences are the **conditions** and the eight strategies are the response.
hr-02 tests that separation, and the most common failure is answering with
strategies.

How each influence reaches an HR decision:

| influence | what it changes | the process or strategy that answers it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| employees as stakeholder | what people will accept and how long they stay | rewards, development, participation |
| unions and employer associations | the terms that can be set at all | dispute resolution, collective bargaining |
| government as legislator | the compliance floor | maintenance, contracts, WHS |
| government as economic manager | the labour market the business hires in | acquisition planning |
| economic cycle | whether labour is scarce or abundant | acquisition, rewards, separation |
| technology | what work exists and where it is done | job design, training, maintenance |
| social change | the working patterns people want | job design, flexible arrangements |
| ethics and CSR | what is acceptable beyond the law | every strategy, at the margin |

**The economic cycle inverts the power relationship**, and this is the most
useful mechanism in the chapter. In an upswing, labour is scarce, employees and
unions hold bargaining power, wages rise and retention becomes the problem. In a
downturn, labour is abundant, employers hold the power, downsizing replaces
recruitment and retention solves itself. So the *same* HR strategy has different
value at different points in the cycle, and an evaluation that ignores the phase
is judging a strategy against the wrong conditions.

**Legal influences are a floor, not a strategy.** WHS, anti-discrimination and
the employment contract set minimums. A business meeting them exactly has
complied and done nothing distinctive, which is the same compliance-against-ethics
structure that runs through operations and marketing. The chapter's own examples
of government as pacesetter, introducing maternity leave and flexitime before the
private sector adopted them, show the space above the floor.

**Stakeholders conflict, and the conflict is the topic.** Employers want
flexibility and cost control; employees want security and balance; unions want
conditions codified; government wants employment and stable prices at once;
society wants both jobs and fairness. HR strategy is a position taken among those
demands, not a solution to them.

## Trade-offs and conditions (DERIVED)

- **Contractors against employees.** Lower on-costs and fewer obligations, at the
  risk that the control test reclassifies the relationship and with less
  commitment from the worker.
- **Casual flexibility against capability.** A casual workforce flexes with
  demand and accumulates less firm-specific skill, and the chapter's own data
  shows many casuals want the security they lack.
- **Compliance cost against risk.** Auditing every policy for indirect
  discrimination costs money; the alternative is a liability that arrives at an
  unpredictable time.
- **Telecommuting against boundary.** Access and productivity against burnout and
  a new duty of care.
- **Wage restraint against retention** in a tight labour market, where the
  chapter's own mechanism says competitors will bid.

## Terminology (SOURCE)

- **stakeholder** — any individual or group with an interest in, or affected by,
  the actions of the business
- **employer** — one who controls employees, pays wages and can dismiss
- **employee** — a worker under an employer's control of location, method and
  supervision
- **employer association** — an organisation representing and assisting employer
  groups, usually a respondent to relevant awards
- **trade union** — an organisation of employees in an industry, trade or
  occupation formed to improve wages and conditions
- **churning** — moving frequently between organisations
- **employment contract** — a legally binding formal agreement between employer
  and employee
- **contract of indefinite duration / fixed term** — continuing until notice, or
  ending at a set date or task completion
- **independent contractor** — self-employed, working under a contract for
  services
- **contract of service / contract for services** — employment, against
  contracting
- **control test** — the court's test of whether a business is in substance the
  employer
- **work health and safety (WHS)** — the framework protecting worker safety
- **workers compensation** — insurance providing medical and financial support to
  injured workers, funded by employer premiums
- **discrimination** — disadvantaging someone for a characteristic irrelevant to
  the work
- **direct / indirect discrimination** — an explicitly unequal rule, against a
  neutral rule with an unequal effect
- **harassment** — offending behaviour or intimidation
- **vilification** — a public discriminatory act inciting hatred
- **equal employment opportunity (EEO)** — ensuring workplace practices give
  everyone equal access
- **labour fragmentation** — the drift from full-time toward part-time work
- **casualisation** — the rising proportion of casual employment
- **virtual team** — a team working together through technology rather than
  location
- **telecommuting** — working remotely using communications technology
- **ethical business practices** — practices that are socially responsible,
  morally right, honourable and fair

## Distinctions and confusions

- **SOURCE. An independent contractor is not an employee.** Different contract,
  different rights, own tax, and possibly several clients.
- **DERIVED. An influence is not a strategy.** hr-02 asks how influences affect
  HRM. Answering with recruitment and training answers hr-01.
- **DERIVED. Direct is not indirect discrimination.** One is an unequal rule; the
  other is an equal rule with unequal effect. The audit obligation exists for the
  second.
- **DERIVED. Part-time is not casual.** Different drivers, different worker
  preference, and the chapter's own evidence says so.
- **DERIVED. A stakeholder is not always external.** Employers and employees are
  stakeholders in their own process, which is what makes the conflicts internal.
- **DERIVED. Government is not one influence.** Five roles, and they can pull
  against each other.
- **DERIVED. Compliance is not good HR management.** It is the floor. Everything
  distinctive happens above it.

## Instructional implications (USE)

Paragraph shape for hr-02, "analyse how key influences affect human resource
management":

1. name the influence and its category, precisely, including which government
   role if it is government
2. state what it changed about the conditions the business works in, not about
   the business's response
3. name the HR process or strategy that had to change as a result
4. state the mechanism connecting them
5. name a second influence that cut across it, or the cost of responding
6. weigh: how far did this influence determine what the business did

Step 2 is the discipline that keeps an influence answer from becoming a strategy
answer. Step 5 is what "analyse" is asking for, since analysis means showing the
relationship between parts rather than listing them.

Candidate pathways: economic cycle to recruitment and retention; technology to
job design and training; casualisation to acquisition and maintenance; WHS
obligations to induction and training; anti-discrimination audit to selection;
union influence to dispute resolution; contractor use to cost and to legal risk.

## Source gaps

- Stakeholders are listed and their conflicting interests are never set out as
  conflicts, though every pair in the list has one.
- The five government roles are given without noting that they can contradict
  each other, except for one aside about economic goals conflicting.
- The economic cycle mechanism is described in one direction, and the inversion of
  bargaining power across the cycle is left for the reader.
- No account of what responding to any influence costs, so every response reads
  as available.
- Ethics and CSR get a definition and a list of consequences with no worked
  instance of a business choosing to go beyond the law.
- Participation rates, wage growth commentary, workforce composition percentages
  and fatality counts are quoted from named bodies without citations a student
  could use. Background for authoring, not evidence.
