## Concepts (SOURCE)

**Cash flow** is the movement of cash into and out of a business over a period.
A cash flow problem exists when more goes out than comes in, or when money must
be paid out before payments owed to the business have arrived. Matching inflow
to outflow in *timing*, not only in total, is the whole task.

Three named strategies: **distribution of payments**, **discounts for early
payment**, **factoring**.

## Explanation (SOURCE)

**Why timing is the problem.** A business can be profitable and still fail,
because profit is recorded when a sale is made and cash arrives when the
customer pays. The receipt of cash does not coincide with the payments falling
due: tax to the ATO, suppliers for accounts payable, wages to employees,
dividends to owners and shareholders, interest and lease payments to financial
institutions. Every one of those has a date attached and none of them waits.

Temporary shortfalls are usually covered by an **overdraft**, where a bank
permits the account to go below zero to a set limit at an agreed interest rate.
A shortfall that persists is a different matter: it can end in insolvency or
bankruptcy.

**Cash flow statements and forecasts.** The statement records receipts and
payments over a period, and because it is a record over time it exposes trends
and can predict change. A **cash flow forecast** estimates expected inflows and
outflows ahead, usually over a year, sometimes a month or a week. Its value is
that it shows surpluses and shortages before they arrive, and shows the likely
cash effect of a decision before the decision is taken.

**The common causes of cash flow trouble** in smaller businesses are worth
knowing because they explain which strategy fits: slow paying debtors, growth
that is rapid or unsustainable, failure to credit-check debtors, tightened
lending making credit harder to get, and seasonality in businesses whose sales
are concentrated in part of the year.

**Distribution of payments.** Spreading payments across the month or year so
large expenses do not fall together. It smooths outflow rather than reducing it,
so the total paid is unchanged and only the timing improves. A forecast is what
makes it possible, because you have to see the peaks before you can flatten
them.

This is the cheapest of the three strategies: it costs nothing but planning, and
it is the only one that does not reduce profit. It is also the weakest, because
it cannot help a business whose inflows are genuinely insufficient.

**Discounts for early payment.** Offering debtors a percentage off the invoice
if they settle before the deadline, for instance two per cent for payment within
ten days. Most effective when aimed at the debtors who owe the most across the
year.

*In favour*: it reduces the risk and cost of late payment, and late payment is
the norm rather than the exception, carrying interest, administrative cost and
time spent chasing. It can build customer loyalty, since the discount is a
reason to choose this supplier. It improves working capital and adds liquidity.
It reduces the risk of non-payment and bad debt.

*Against*: the discount comes straight out of profit margin. It can make cash
flow harder to forecast rather than easier, because you no longer know when
payment will land. It requires careful tracking, or the business gives discounts
to customers who claim to have paid early and did not, and recovering the
shortfall damages the relationship. And nothing guarantees customers keep paying
early once the habit is established.

**Factoring.** Selling accounts receivable to a finance or specialist factoring
company at a discount. The business gets cash now and is relieved of chasing the
debt.

*In favour*: cash can arrive within a day, which improves working capital
immediately. It is not a loan, so it adds no debt and no interest. It is quick to
arrange with no lengthy application. It frees the owner from debt collection.
Customers may pay a factor more promptly than they pay a supplier. And
availability depends on the customers' credit ratings rather than the business's,
so a business with poor credit can still use it.

*Against*: it reduces the profit margin on every invoice sold. It can be more
expensive than other short-term finance. It can damage customer relationships,
because the customer now deals with a third party, especially if that party
collects aggressively. And it signals to customers that the business may have
cash flow problems, which can make them wary of dealing with it at all.

**Invoice discounting** is the near-identical alternative worth knowing: same
principle, but the business continues to chase its own payments, so customers
never learn of the arrangement. It removes the reputational objection and keeps
the collection workload.

**The ethical edge.** Cash flow management has a victim when it is done at a
supplier's expense. Extending payment terms to ninety days improves the paying
business's cash flow by worsening its suppliers'. Where small suppliers then
have to take a discount to be paid earlier, the larger business's liquidity has
been financed by the smaller ones. The source records large businesses
abandoning such programs under public pressure, which makes this a genuine CSR
point inside a finance topic.

## Relationships and mechanisms (DERIVED unless marked)

Cash flow management is a **cause** acting on the financial objectives, and it
acts most directly on **liquidity**. Its distinguishing feature is that two of
the three strategies buy liquidity with profitability, and saying so is the
argument:

| strategy | objective raised | objective paid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| distribution of payments | liquidity | nothing directly; the limit is that it cannot create cash that does not exist |
| discounts for early payment | liquidity, and reduced bad debt risk | profitability, since the discount comes from margin; and forecastability |
| factoring | liquidity, immediately, with no new debt | profitability on every invoice sold, and the customer relationship |

Factoring's cleanest advantage is one students miss: it raises liquidity
**without raising gearing**, because it is a sale of an asset rather than a
borrowing. A business worried about solvency can use factoring where it could not
use a loan. That is a real link between a cash flow strategy and the solvency
objective, and it is the sort of connection fin-01 rewards.

Cash flow management also links to:

- **working capital management**, since accounts receivable are a current asset
  and all three strategies act on them
- **efficiency**, since collecting receivables faster is one of the stated
  efficiency controls
- **operations**, since inventory ties up cash and JIT frees it
- **marketing**, since credit terms are part of what is being sold

## Terminology (SOURCE)

- **cash flow** — movement of cash in and out over a period
- **cash inflow** — sales, receipts from accounts receivable, commissions, asset
  sales, share issue proceeds, interest and dividends received
- **cash outflow** — payments to suppliers, loan interest, operating expenses
  including wages, drawings, asset purchases, loan repayments
- **cash flow statement** — a record of receipts and payments over a period
- **cash flow forecast** — an estimate of expected inflows and outflows ahead
- **overdraft** — a bank facility permitting an account to be overdrawn to a set
  limit at interest
- **distribution of payments** — spreading outflows so large expenses do not
  coincide
- **discount for early payment** — a percentage reduction for settling an invoice
  before the deadline
- **factoring** — selling accounts receivable at a discount to a finance company
- **invoice discounting** — the same, but the business continues to collect, so
  customers are unaware
- **accounts receivable / debtors** — money owed to the business by customers
- **accounts payable** — money the business owes its suppliers
- **bad debt** — an amount owed that will not be collected
- **insolvency** — inability to meet financial obligations as they fall due

## Distinctions and confusions

- **Cash flow is not profit.** A profitable business can run out of cash and a
  business with strong cash flow can be making a loss. This is the foundational
  distinction of the topic and most errors trace back to it.
- **Factoring is not a loan.** It is a sale of an asset. It adds no debt and no
  interest, which is why it does not raise gearing.
- **Factoring is not invoice discounting.** The difference is who chases the
  debt, and therefore whether the customer knows.
- **Distribution of payments does not increase cash.** It moves outflows in
  time. Total cash out is identical.
- **A discount is not free.** It is paid out of profit margin on every invoice
  that takes it, including invoices that would have been paid on time anyway.
- **Improving your cash flow can worsen someone else's.** Extending payment
  terms is a cash flow strategy whose cost falls on the supplier.
- **An overdraft is a symptom, not a strategy.** It covers a temporary shortfall
  at a cost. Persistent reliance on one is the signal of a structural problem.

## Instructional implications (USE)

1. name the cash flow problem precisely, including its timing: what has to be
   paid, and when the money for it arrives
2. name the strategy chosen
3. state the mechanism: which flow moves, in which direction, by how much,
   and when
4. name the cost, in margin, in relationship, or in signal
5. judge whether it fixed the timing problem or only postponed it

Step 5 is the distinction between a real answer and a list. Factoring and early
payment discounts pull future cash forward. If the underlying inflow is
insufficient rather than merely late, they buy time and cost margin, which makes
the position worse the following period.
