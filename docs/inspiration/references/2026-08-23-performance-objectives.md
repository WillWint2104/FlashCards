## Concepts (SOURCE)

Performance objectives are the goals a business sets for the parts of its
transformation process it has decided matter. There are six: quality, speed,
dependability, flexibility, customisation and cost. Each is expressed as a target
and measured against it, which is what makes them usable as KPIs rather than
aspirations.

## Explanation (SOURCE)

Each objective names a different thing an operation can be good at.

| objective | what it measures | measured by |
| --- | --- | --- |
| quality | whether the product matches what it was designed to be, and whether that design matches what customers wanted | warranty claims, defect rate, complaints |
| speed | how fast the process turns demand into a delivered product | lead time, waiting time, processing time |
| dependability | whether the product or service is consistent, every time | warranty claims for goods, complaint volume for services |
| flexibility | how fast the process can change when the market changes | time to change capacity, time to add a product line |
| customisation | how far the product can be made to one customer's requirement | proportion of output individually specified |
| cost | what it costs to run the process at all | cost per unit, waste, holding cost |

Quality splits three ways and the split matters: **design** (is the specification
worth wanting), **conformance** (does output actually hit the specification, whatever
that specification is), and **service** (is delivery reliable and responsive). The
second is the one students misread. A cheap plastic toy that meets its own low
specification has *high* conformance quality. Conformance measures consistency
against a standard, not the height of the standard.

## Relationships and mechanisms (DERIVED unless marked)

This is the part the source states least and the part the app needs most.

**The six objectives compete.** A business cannot maximise all of them, and the
source concedes this only in its closing sentence: a business allocates resources
to the objective most likely to serve profitability. Everything interesting about
this topic follows from that sentence, so it should be the *first* thing a student
meets, not the last.

The tensions worth authoring as pathways:

- **customisation against cost.** Full customisation costs significantly more than
  standardised production, so it is offered only where the product adapts cheaply
  or where the whole business model is built on it. Mass customisation is the
  compromise: standardise the item, vary a constrained set of options.
- **speed against cost.** Removing bottlenecks means spare capacity, training, or
  an alliance to absorb overflow demand — all of which cost money to hold idle.
- **flexibility against cost.** Flexibility is bought with capacity, new plant, or
  broader product design. Each is capital the business is not using elsewhere.
- **quality of design against cost.** Higher-grade inputs raise the price, and some
  customers will not pay it. Choosing design quality is choosing a market segment.
- **speed and flexibility reinforce.** The faster the processing time, the more
  quickly the process can be adjusted. These two pull together, not apart, and a
  student who assumes every pair trades off will get this one wrong.

Performance objectives are the **effect** end of every operations argument. The
operations strategies — inventory, quality management, supply chain, technology,
outsourcing — are the **cause** end. That is the shape `ops-01` asks for, and it
is what a `reasoning` block for this question should encode: strategy vocabulary
on the cause side, objective vocabulary on the effect side.

## Terminology (SOURCE)

- **performance objective** — a goal set for an aspect of the transformation process
- **quality of design** — how good the specification is
- **quality of conformance** — how consistently output meets that specification
- **quality of service** — reliability and responsiveness of delivery
- **dependability** — consistency; also called reliability
- **flexibility** — also called adaptability
- **mass customisation** — a standard mass-produced item modified to a customer's
  requirements within a constrained set of options
- **constrained customisation** — the same idea named from the limit side: choices
  are deliberately restricted so the process can still absorb the variation
- **bottleneck** — a point that limits throughput. **Procedural** when a process
  exists and is not followed; **technical** when capacity itself is the limit. The
  distinction matters because the fixes differ: training against procedural,
  capacity or an alliance against technical.

## Distinctions and confusions

- **Conformance is not excellence.** High conformance to a low specification is
  still high conformance. Students routinely read "quality of conformance" as
  "high quality".
- **Customisation is not flexibility.** Customisation is about the product varying
  per customer; flexibility is about the process changing when the market does. A
  business can be highly flexible and sell one standardised product.
- **Speed is not dependability.** Fast and inconsistent is a real and common
  failure state. Dependability is about variance, speed is about the mean.
- **Cost is an objective, not a residual.** Students treat cost as whatever is left
  after the other choices. It is a target with its own KPIs, and choosing it as
  the priority is a strategic position, not an absence of one.

## Instructional implications (USE)

The shape that makes a paragraph work here:

1. name the objective the business has chosen to prioritise
2. name the strategy it uses to pursue it
3. state the mechanism — how the strategy actually moves that objective
4. name what the choice costs against a competing objective
5. say why the trade was worth making for this business

Step 4 is the one the source omits and the one that separates a band 4 answer
from a band 6 one on a judgement question. A paragraph that reaches step 3 and
stops has explained; a paragraph that reaches step 5 has assessed.
