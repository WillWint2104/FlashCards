## Concepts (SOURCE)

Four processes, forming a cycle:

| process | definition |
| --- | --- |
| **acquisition** | attracting and recruiting the right staff for roles |
| **development** | enhancing skills in line with the changing and future needs of the business |
| **maintenance** | managing staff needs for health and safety, industrial relations and legal responsibilities, including compensation and benefits |
| **separation** | employees leaving voluntarily, or through dismissal or retrenchment |

The chapter states the connection hr-01 and hr-03 both need: the effectiveness of
HRM in turnover, absenteeism, disputation and worker satisfaction is **directly
aligned with how these four processes are handled**. It also notes that
acquisition and development in particular may be outsourced.

## Explanation (SOURCE)

**Acquisition** begins with analysis, not advertising.

*Internal environment*: goals and objectives, current performance, corporate
culture, technology, structure, size, rates of turnover and promotion,
productivity levels, and budget. The business's focus, whether cost containment,
growth, downsizing, customer service or quality, determines what skills are
demanded now and later, and which people fit the culture.

*External environment*: competition, seasonal labour market trends, economic
trends affecting sales, labour costs, changes in legislation affecting human
resources, and changes in industrial relations practice.

Then the planning sequence: forecast **demand** (turnover, number, skill, tasks
from job analysis, experience), forecast **supply** (the human resources
inventory of numbers, qualifications, skills, occupations, performance,
experience, turnover rates and career goals), determine the variance, and develop
objectives and strategies.

The response depends on the variance:

| variance | response |
| --- | --- |
| shortage | increase overtime and casuals, stop retirements, accelerate training and development, start recruiting, outsource |
| surplus | reduce overtime and casuals, encourage early retirement with retrenchment packages, stop recruiting, reduce working hours |
| nil | change some other aspect of employment relations, such as training or workplace culture |

Job analysis and job design then produce job specifications and descriptions,
developed through staff interviews, observation and performance appraisal
reports.

The chapter also names three job design responses to the boredom and
dissatisfaction that specialisation causes, all based on **intrinsic rewards**,
the good feeling from doing something well:

- **job rotation** — switching between jobs for a period, giving variety and a
  more comprehensive view of the business
- **job enlargement** — more things to do within the same job, giving variety and
  challenge
- **job enrichment** — more control and independence over how the work is done,
  making it more interesting and providing self-satisfaction

**Development** retains experienced and talented staff and raises motivation and
commitment through longer-term promotion opportunity. Employees who feel
competent and are recognised for achievement are more motivated, more satisfied
and perform better.

Development needs change across a career: early on, gaining qualifications; then
experiencing a variety of roles to find interests and talents; later, specialist
or managerial competencies for senior positions, with talented staff sometimes
fast-tracked; and for employees over forty, upgrading knowledge, mentoring,
work-life balance and transition to retirement.

Its components are further professional learning, mentoring or coaching, and
performance appraisal and management. The HR manager's role is to establish
programs, train managers to deliver them, and **evaluate whether performance
actually improved as a result**.

*Induction* introduces a new employee to the job, colleagues, the business and
its culture. The chapter gives the reason it matters: most employees who leave a
business depart in the first three months, so support is needed most in a new
job. A good induction gives a positive attitude, builds confidence, stresses
safety policy and its application, and establishes working relationships.

*Training* seeks long-term change. Multiskilled employees adapt better to rapid
technological change, are more productive, and gain promotion opportunities. Many
enterprise agreements pursue **functional flexibility** through multiskilling,
job rotation, removal of restrictive work practices, reduced classifications and
demarcations, job enlargement and job enrichment.

**Maintenance** retains staff and manages wellbeing: safety and health, effective
communication, and compliance with industrial agreements and legal
responsibilities. Wellbeing is maximised by encouraging participation in decision
making and giving employees some control over their working lives.

*Communication*: effective workplace relationships depend heavily on
communication systems, and poor communication shows as workplace conflict and
high turnover. Methods include team meetings, bulletins and newsletters,
seminars, social functions, suggestion boxes and staff surveys, email and
intranet. The chapter singles out email as a source of misunderstanding and
tension without protocols and feedback timelines, and names the expectation that
staff attend to email at home as a common source of conflict requiring management
to avoid burnout and grievances.

*Employee participation* improves communication, empowers employees and builds
commitment to quality and efficiency, with staff trained to make some decisions
on the spot as customers demand faster service. Its value depends on the
training, knowledge and skills of those involved, and the chapter is pointed
about quality: suggestion boxes are often cited as participation, but regular
team meetings discussing customer feedback, trends and issues are more effective
because they build shared purpose and identity.

Strategies, with their stated problems:

| strategy | note |
| --- | --- |
| board membership | presents conflicts of interest; the representative often becomes alienated from other employees |
| ownership through shares | may raise commitment, but ownership and participation have different objectives and can conflict, especially at scale |
| joint consultative committees | formal groups of employee and management representatives, mandatory for WHS in larger NSW workplaces |
| collective bargaining | a majority of employees must be involved in developing an enterprise agreement; good faith bargaining requires all parties to try to resolve issues by negotiation and conciliation |
| team briefings | sharing knowledge and skills, finding solutions, developing innovations |
| surveys and performance interview feedback | seeking feedback on improving business performance |

*Benefits* are described as a litmus test of workplace culture because they are
available to all staff. They may be monetary or non-monetary and their extent
reflects the business's resources and activities. Legally mandated benefits
include employer superannuation contributions under the Superannuation Guarantee,
with stated exclusions and record-keeping obligations. Family-friendly programs
supporting work-life balance include job sharing, multiskilling, telecommuting,
part-time work and flexible hours. Employee assistance programs provide
confidential support for personal problems affecting performance and wellbeing.

**Separation** may be voluntary (resignation, relocation, voluntary redundancy,
retirement) or involuntary (contract expiry, retrenchment, dismissal). The
chapter's framing is that involuntary separation must be managed carefully to
avoid discrimination claims and adverse effects on the morale and productivity of
**remaining** staff, and that managing separation ethically, transparently and
fairly maintains the trust and commitment of those who stay.

## Load-bearing derived claims (DERIVED)

```
SOURCE  Separation must be handled ethically and fairly to maintain the trust and
        commitment of remaining staff, and to avoid adverse effects on their morale
        and productivity.
DERIVED Separation is the only one of the four processes whose main audience is not
        the person it acts on. Its business effect runs through everyone who stays
        and watches how it was done, which is why a legally sound dismissal handled
        badly still damages performance.
USE     The strongest available answer to why separation is a process rather than an
        administrative end point, and the step that makes hr-03 answerable.
```

```
SOURCE  Most employees who leave a business depart in the first three months, so the
        need for support is greatest in a new job.
DERIVED That single fact links acquisition, development and separation into one
        problem. Early turnover is usually an acquisition failure (a mismatch that
        should have been screened out) or a development failure (an induction that
        did not build confidence), and it is counted as a separation statistic.
USE     Lets a student diagnose a turnover figure by asking when people left, not
        just how many, which is a genuinely analytical move.
```

```
SOURCE  Acquisition begins with forecasting demand and supply and determining the
        variance, with distinct responses for shortage and surplus.
DERIVED Acquisition is therefore not a synonym for recruitment. Recruiting is one of
        five responses to a shortage, and none of the responses to a surplus involve
        it. A business managing a surplus is doing acquisition work while hiring
        nobody.
USE     A precision correction that unlocks the whole process, since students treat
        acquisition and recruitment as the same word.
```

```
SOURCE  The chapter says suggestion boxes are often cited as employee participation
        but that regular team meetings discussing customer feedback and trends are
        more effective, and that participation's value depends on the training and
        knowledge of those involved.
DERIVED Participation is not a single strategy with one effect. Its return scales
        with what participants know, which is why participation and development are
        conditional on each other: consultation with untrained staff produces the
        appearance of involvement without its benefit.
USE     Gives an evaluate answer a condition rather than an assertion that
        participation is good.
```

```
SOURCE  Board membership creates conflicts of interest and can alienate the
        representative from other employees. Employee ownership can conflict because
        ownership and participation have different objectives.
DERIVED The chapter states a cost for the two deepest forms of participation and none
        for the shallow ones. The pattern is that participation's cost rises with its
        depth, so a business chooses how far to go rather than whether to do it.
USE     A structured way to compare participation strategies instead of listing them.
```

## Relationships and mechanisms (DERIVED unless marked)

The four processes are a **cycle**, and the most useful thing to teach about them
is that a failure in one appears as a symptom in another.

```
acquisition  ->  development  ->  maintenance  ->  separation
     ^                                                  |
     +--------------------------------------------------+
```

| symptom appears in | cause often sits in |
| --- | --- |
| early separation, first three months | acquisition (poor screening) or development (weak induction) |
| low productivity despite good hires | development (training not evaluated) |
| disputation and grievances | maintenance (communication systems) |
| difficulty acquiring staff | separation (reputation of how leavers were treated) |
| high absenteeism | maintenance (wellbeing, participation, work-life balance) |

That last arrow is the one the chapter never draws and the one that closes the
cycle: how a business separates people determines how easily it acquires them.

**Processes against strategies.** hr-03 asks about processes and hr-01 asks about
strategies, and students conflate them. The distinction that works: a **process**
is a stage every employee passes through, and a **strategy** is a choice about how
to run it. Recruitment is a strategy; acquisition is the process it sits inside.
Training and development is a strategy; development is the process. Rewards is a
strategy; maintenance is the process.

**Processes against effectiveness.** The chapter states the link directly, which
is unusual for this textbook: turnover, absenteeism, disputation and satisfaction
are aligned with how the four processes are handled. That gives hr-03 its
structure, because "to what extent can HR processes contribute to effective HRM"
is asking exactly this.

**Maintenance is the process with the most legal content**, since it carries WHS,
industrial agreements, superannuation and payroll obligations. It is therefore
where compliance and strategy meet, and where the compliance floor is highest.

**Outsourcing changes which processes the business controls.** The chapter notes
acquisition and development may be outsourced. A business that outsources both
retains maintenance and separation, which are the two with the highest legal
exposure and the two that most affect remaining staff.

## Trade-offs and conditions (DERIVED)

- **Screening cost against early turnover cost.** More thorough acquisition costs
  time and reduces first-three-months separation. Which is cheaper depends on the
  cost of a bad hire in that role.
- **Development against retention risk.** Development raises capability and
  marketability at once; the chapter's answer is that promotion opportunity is the
  retention mechanism, which only works if the opportunity is real.
- **Participation depth against speed and cohesion.** Deeper participation gives
  better decisions and slower ones, and at board and ownership level creates the
  conflicts the chapter names.
- **Flexible work against coordination.** Job sharing, telecommuting and flexible
  hours support retention and complicate rostering, handover and communication.
- **Fair separation against speed and cost.** Careful process protects morale and
  reduces claims, and takes longer.

## Terminology (SOURCE)

- **acquisition** — attracting and recruiting the right staff
- **development** — enhancing skills for the changing and future needs of the
  business
- **maintenance** — managing health and safety, industrial relations and legal
  responsibilities including compensation and benefits
- **separation** — employees leaving voluntarily or involuntarily
- **human resources inventory** — the record of current staff numbers,
  qualifications, skills, performance and career goals
- **job analysis** — determining the tasks a position requires
- **job specification / job description** — what the person needs, and what the
  job involves
- **job rotation / job enlargement / job enrichment** — switching jobs for
  variety; more tasks at the same level; more control and independence
- **intrinsic reward** — the satisfaction from doing something well
- **induction** — introducing a new employee to the job, colleagues and culture
- **multiskilling** — training an employee across several roles
- **functional flexibility** — the capacity to move staff between tasks and roles
- **employee participation** — involving employees in decision making
- **joint consultative committee** — a formal group of employee and management
  representatives; mandatory for WHS in larger NSW workplaces
- **collective bargaining** — negotiating an enterprise agreement, requiring
  majority employee involvement
- **good faith bargaining** — parties must make an effort to resolve issues
  through negotiation and conciliation
- **Superannuation Guarantee** — the employer's compulsory contribution to an
  employee's retirement fund
- **employee assistance program (EAP)** — confidential support for personal
  problems affecting work
- **voluntary separation** — resignation, relocation, voluntary redundancy,
  retirement
- **involuntary separation** — contract expiry, retrenchment, dismissal
- **retrenchment** — separation because the position is no longer needed

## Distinctions and confusions

- **SOURCE. Job enlargement is not job enrichment.** Enlargement adds tasks at
  the same level; enrichment adds control and independence. The chapter defines
  both, in the same list, which makes this the one place the textbook is clearer
  than students expect.
- **DERIVED. Acquisition is not recruitment.** Recruitment is one of five
  responses to a forecast shortage.
- **DERIVED. A process is not a strategy.** Processes are stages every employee
  passes through; strategies are choices about how to run them. This is the
  hr-01 against hr-03 distinction.
- **DERIVED. Voluntary separation is not automatically good.** Losing a
  high-performer voluntarily is worse than a well-managed retrenchment. The
  composition matters more than the category.
- **DERIVED. Retrenchment is not dismissal.** Retrenchment ends a position;
  dismissal ends an employment because of conduct or performance. They carry
  different obligations.
- **DERIVED. Maintenance is not maintenance of equipment.** It is retention and
  wellbeing, and the word misleads almost every student who meets it first here.
- **DERIVED. Participation is not consultation about anything.** Its value is
  conditional on the participants' knowledge, which the chapter states and most
  answers ignore.

## Instructional implications (USE)

Paragraph shape for hr-03, "to what extent can HR processes contribute to
effective HRM":

1. name the process, and be precise that it is a process rather than a strategy
2. name what the business actually does at that stage
3. state the mechanism reaching an effectiveness indicator
4. name the indicator, with its benchmark
5. name what limits the contribution: a cost, a legal floor, or another process
   that has to work for this one to pay off
6. judge the extent, which means saying what the process cannot fix on its own

Step 6 is the command word. "To what extent" is answered by naming the boundary,
and the boundary here is usually another process: acquisition cannot fix a
retention problem caused by maintenance, and development cannot retain people if
the promotion opportunity it promises does not exist.

Candidate pathways: induction quality to first-three-months separation; demand
and supply forecasting to overtime and casual cost; participation depth to
disputation; separation handling to remaining-staff productivity and to future
acquisition; benefits to absenteeism.

## Adjacent concepts

Strategies in human resource management, which are the choices inside these
processes; effectiveness of HRM and its seven indicators; key influences on HRM,
particularly the legal framework in maintenance; overcoming resistance to change
in operations; corporate social responsibility in separation; the people element
of the extended marketing mix.

## Source gaps

- The cycle is drawn as four boxes and the feedback from separation back to
  acquisition is never stated, though the chapter supplies both halves.
- The first-three-months finding is given as a reason for induction and never
  connected to the separation process it produces.
- No treatment of what any process costs, so the whole chapter reads as things a
  business should simply do.
- Employee participation strategies are listed with costs attached to two of six
  and nothing said about choosing between them.
- Superannuation rates, exclusion thresholds and similar figures are quoted at a
  point in time and change. Background for authoring, not evidence.
