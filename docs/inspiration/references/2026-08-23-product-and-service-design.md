## Definition

Designing and developing new goods and services is an operations strategy
because the design, development, launch and sale of new products is how a
business grows and gains competitive advantage. It is the one operations
strategy that decides *what* is produced rather than how well.

## Explanation

**Two approaches, and the difference is where the idea comes from.**

- **Consumer-driven.** Market research identifies what customers prefer, and
  those preferences determine what gets designed. The business finds a need and
  builds for it.
- **Technology-driven.** A change or innovation in technology makes a new
  product possible, and the product finds its market afterwards because the
  advanced technique gives it greater functionality.

Neither is the correct one. They suit different businesses and different
markets, and a student who can name which one a business used has said something
worth marks. The distinction also explains why some businesses keep design in
house rather than outsourcing it to save cost: control over both hardware and
software is what makes a technology-driven approach possible at all.

**The design and development process**, in stages:

1. market research, product concept, and specification development
2. product design and prototype development, with the quality parameters decided
3. prototype testing and assessment, including market testing
4. product refinement, and refinement of the production processes to make it
5. production, launch, distribution, and over time extension of the product line

Stage 2 is the one to point out: quality parameters are decided at design, not
at inspection. That is the connection to quality assurance, and it is what
"right first time" actually means in practice.

**Four factors run through every stage.**

| factor | why it constrains the design |
| --- | --- |
| quality | the market expects particular attributes and features, and the design has to carry them |
| supply chain management | a new product draws on suppliers, and may widen the range, change the timing, or raise the volume of supplies needed |
| output capacity | the product may consume existing resources, widen the range of resources used, or require investment in new capital equipment |
| cost | determined by the quantity and type of inputs, and by the time and energy consumed in processing |

**Value** is related to cost but is not the same thing, because value includes
the customer's perception of **product utility**: the usefulness and value the
product has from the consumer's point of view. Cost is what the business spends;
utility is what the customer thinks they got.

**Service design differs.** It starts from the customer, because services are
customised in nature and are consumed as they are produced. The degree of
customisation varies: some services involve little or no customer interaction
and so are largely standardised, while professional services customise to the
individual client. The shelf packer and the short-order cook sit at one end; the
accountant, lawyer, specialist and journalist at the other.

Designing a service means settling three things:

- **explicit service** — the tangible part: the time, expertise, skill and
  effort applied
- **implicit service** — the intangible part: the psychological wellbeing, the
  feeling of being looked after
- **the goods required** to deliver it: a surgeon needs sutures and a theatre, a
  tutor needs books and a computer

Cost is determined once those features are settled, not before.

The point worth teaching hard is that implicit service has to be **designed
in**, not hoped for. The skill, time and expertise are embedded in the design so
that when the service is delivered the customer knows they were specifically
catered for. Implicit satisfaction is an outcome of a design decision, not of
staff being nice.

## Relationships to other concepts

This strategy is unusual in the syllabus because it is a **cause of the other
strategies** rather than a peer of them. A design decision commits the business
to a supply chain, a capacity, a quality standard and a cost structure, all
before anything is made. The four factors listed above are literally the other
operations topics appearing as design constraints.

Against performance objectives:

- design sets **quality of design**, by definition; it is the objective's
  namesake
- it sets the ceiling on **cost**, because inputs and processing time are chosen
  at design and can only be trimmed afterwards
- it determines how much **customisation** is possible, since a design that
  admits variation is a different design from one that does not
- it constrains **flexibility**, because a product designed around specific
  equipment locks the process to that equipment

Service design connects straight into marketing's extended mix: explicit service
is close to the processes element, implicit service is close to people, and the
goods required to deliver a service overlap with physical evidence. A student
who notices that can move between topics with real content rather than a
gesture.

Technology-driven design is where this topic meets leading edge technology and
research and development. Consumer-driven design is where it meets market
research and target markets.

## Terminology

- **product design and development** — creating new goods and services for the
  operations process
- **specification** — the definition of what the product must be, settled before
  design
- **prototype** — a first working version, built to be tested
- **market testing** — assessing a prototype with actual customers before
  committing to production
- **product line extension** — adding variants to an existing product over time
- **product utility** — the usefulness and value a product has from the
  consumer's point of view
- **value** — related to cost, but including the customer's perception of
  utility
- **explicit service** — the tangible part of a service: time, expertise, skill,
  effort
- **implicit service** — the intangible part: the feeling of being looked after
- **standardised service** — a service with little or no customer interaction,
  delivered the same way each time
- **customised service** — a service adapted to the individual client

## Distinctions and confusions

- **Explicit is not the same as good.** Explicit service is the tangible input.
  A service can be high in explicit service and low in implicit service, which
  is the technically competent professional the client never wants to see again.
- **Product design is not product differentiation.** Design is an operations
  activity that creates the product. Differentiation is a marketing position
  taken with it. They are frequently the same decision seen from two functions,
  and saying which function you are speaking as is what earns the mark.
- **Quality parameters are set at design.** Not at inspection. Quality control
  checks against a standard that this process already decided.
- **Value is not cost.** Cost is what the business spends. Value includes what
  the customer perceives. A business can raise value without raising cost, and
  spend without adding any.
- **Consumer-driven is not automatically safer.** It builds for a need that
  already exists, which also means competitors can see it. Technology-driven
  design creates a market nobody else is in yet, at the risk that nobody wants
  it.
- **Services are designed, not improvised.** Students treat service quality as a
  staffing outcome. The source is explicit that implicit service is embedded in
  the design.

## Worked example, structurally

1. name the product or service and which approach produced it, consumer-driven
   or technology-driven
2. name the design decision precisely, and for a service split it into explicit
   and implicit
3. name the performance objective it was aiming at
4. state the mechanism: what about the design produces that outcome
5. name what the design committed the business to, in supply chain, capacity or
   cost, and whether that commitment was worth making

Step 5 is what makes this a strategy paragraph rather than a description of a
product. The design is the moment the business's options narrow, and saying what
narrowed is the analysis.

## What the source does not give us

- No trade-off between the two approaches. Both are described and neither is
  costed, so there is no basis for saying which suits which business.
- No link from design decisions to named performance objectives, though quality
  of design is defined a few sections earlier under exactly that name.
- Service design gets the explicit and implicit distinction and then no method
  for designing implicit service beyond the assertion that it must be embedded.
- The company examples illustrate that design matters. None documents a design
  decision, its constraint, and its result, which is what a worked example would
  need to be.
