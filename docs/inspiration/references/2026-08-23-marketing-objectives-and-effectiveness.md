## Concepts (SOURCE)

**Marketing objectives** are the realistic and measurable goals to be achieved
through the marketing plan. They should align with the overall business goals
but be more customer-oriented, and they concern products and markets.

Three named objectives:

| objective | definition |
| --- | --- |
| **increasing market share** | raising the business's share of total industry sales for a product |
| **expanding the product mix** | widening the total range of products offered |
| **maximising customer service** | responding to the needs and problems of the customer |

Effectiveness is judged by **monitoring** (measuring actual against planned
performance) and **controlling** (comparing the two and taking corrective
action), using three performance indicators: **sales analysis**, **market share
analysis** and **marketing return on investment**.

## Explanation (SOURCE)

**Objectives must carry a number.** The chapter is explicit that objectives
should include specific targets, such as increasing market share by ten per cent
within twelve months, and that deciding what the business wants to achieve is the
most important step in the planning process. An objective without a figure and a
deadline cannot be monitored, which makes it not an objective.

**Market share** is the business's share of total industry sales for a product.
Businesses pursue it with wide product ranges and multiple brand names to gain a
few percentage points. It matters most to businesses already dominating a market,
because small gains translate into large profits.

**Product mix** is the total range offered. Businesses expand it because the same
mix does not stay effective: tastes and preferences change and demand for
particular products falls. Each item in a line should serve a different target
market. The chapter also carries the counter-case, a retailer whose strategy is a
deliberately narrow range of some fifteen hundred lines against twenty to thirty
thousand in a full-range competitor, which delivers a smaller store, lower
warehousing cost and supplier discounts that fund lower prices.

**Customer service** is described as perhaps the most important of the three. It
produces satisfaction, positive reaction, a sound customer base and repeat
purchase. The strategies listed: ask customers what they want; train and reward
employees for excellent service; anticipate trends by research; check what
competitors offer and review the product mix; build long-term relationships; and
encourage staff to be customer-oriented rather than sales-oriented. The chapter
frames service as an attitude adopted by everyone in the business rather than a
refund policy or a complaints desk.

**Implementation** is putting the strategies into operation, the how, where and
when after the plan has said what and why. It asks whether the plan is integrated
with the rest of the business, how the business should be structured, whether
communication lines exist between marketing and other departments, who is best
for each task, and whether marketing staff and all other employees know and are
motivated by the objectives.

**Monitoring** is checking and observing actual progress, with staff gathering
information and reporting changes, problems and opportunities during the life of
the plan.

**Controlling** compares planned against actual performance and takes corrective
action. It runs on two questions: what are the objectives, and are they being
achieved. Step one is to establish a **key performance indicator**, a forecast
level of performance against which actual performance can be compared, such as
increasing monthly sales by five per cent. Step two is to compare actual
performance against it using budgets, sales statistics and cost analyses. The
chapter states that only by establishing KPIs and comparing actual performance
can a manager evaluate the effectiveness of the plan.

**Financial forecast.** Before choosing between strategies, the business forecasts
costs and revenues for each and compares them by **cost-benefit analysis**. Costs
divide into market research, product development, promotion including advertising
and packaging, and distribution. Revenue is forecast from what consumers are
expected to buy and at what price, plus what sales staff predict. The chapter
concedes two limits: results are open to individual interpretation, and costs are
easier to forecast than revenue because costs are largely controlled by the
business while revenue depends on an external environment it cannot control.

**The three performance indicators.**

- **Sales analysis** compares actual sales with forecast sales, and gets clearer
  the further the data is broken down, for instance by territory against quota.
- **Market share analysis** compares the business's position against competitors,
  and separates internal from external causes. If sales revenue falls while
  market share holds, industry sales have fallen and the cause is external. If
  both fall, the strategies need review.
- **Marketing return on investment** measures revenue generated against campaign
  cost: sales growth minus marketing cost, divided by marketing cost. The chapter
  offers five to one as a good marketing ROI.

**Revising the strategy.** Once results are known, the plan is modified. Changes
run across the mix: product modifications to hold competitive advantage, price
modifications in response to the external environment, promotion modifications
tracking the product life cycle (high at launch, stabilising and falling in
decline), and place modifications as markets grow, shift or move online. Beyond
the mix sit **new product development**, since products have finite lives and
long-term growth requires new ones, and **product deletion**, eliminating lines
because outdated products create an unfavourable image that can affect the rest of
the range.

## Load-bearing derived claims (DERIVED)

```
SOURCE  Market share analysis separates internal from external causes: falling sales
        with stable share means industry sales fell, while falling sales and falling
        share means the strategies need review.
DERIVED This is the only tool in the chapter that can tell a student whether a
        strategy worked. Sales analysis alone cannot, because a rise in sales during a
        boom and a fall during a recession may both be independent of anything
        marketing did.
USE     The core of mkt-02. An answer judging effectiveness on sales figures alone has
        not controlled for the market, and saying so is the single most valuable move
        available on an effectiveness question.
```

```
SOURCE  Objectives should include specific targets. A KPI is a forecast level of
        performance against which actual performance is compared. Only by comparing
        the two can effectiveness be evaluated.
DERIVED Effectiveness is therefore not a property of a strategy, it is a relationship
        between a result and a target set beforehand. A strategy that raised sales
        while missing its objective was not effective, and one that lost sales more
        slowly than the market may have been.
USE     Reframes "assess the effectiveness" from "was it good" to "against what, and
        was that target the right one", which is what the command word requires.
```

```
SOURCE  Costs are easier to forecast than revenue because costs are largely controlled
        by the business while revenue depends on an external environment it cannot
        control. Cost-benefit results are also open to individual interpretation.
DERIVED So the denominator of marketing ROI is reliable and the numerator is not.
        Every effectiveness measure in this chapter is more confident about what was
        spent than about what it achieved.
USE     The limitation step for any answer that quotes a return figure, and a genuine
        reason to prefer market share analysis to a ratio.
```

```
SOURCE  Expanding the product mix is given as a marketing objective. A case in the
        same section describes a retailer succeeding through a deliberately narrow
        range that lowers warehousing cost and wins supplier discounts.
DERIVED The objective and the counter-case contradict each other, and the chapter does
        not notice. Range expansion serves more target markets and raises cost and
        complexity; range restriction serves one target market and funds lower prices.
        Which is right depends on whether the business competes on choice or on price.
USE     Lets a student argue that a marketing objective was wrongly chosen, which is
        the strongest form an evaluation can take.
```

## Relationships and mechanisms (DERIVED unless marked)

Objectives are the **effect** end of a marketing argument and strategies are the
**cause** end, which is the same structure as ops-01 and fin-01. mkt-02 adds a
third element: the **measure**, which is what makes it an effectiveness question
rather than a strategy question.

```
strategy  ->  mechanism  ->  objective  ->  indicator that would show it
```

A paragraph that stops at the objective has explained. A paragraph that names how
anyone would know has assessed.

Which indicator fits which objective:

| objective | the indicator that actually tests it | why the obvious one fails |
| --- | --- | --- |
| increasing market share | market share analysis | sales can rise while share falls in a growing market |
| expanding the product mix | sales analysis broken down by line, plus ROI per line | total sales hide a new line cannibalising an old one |
| maximising customer service | repeat purchase and retention over time | a satisfaction score measures the attitude, not the behaviour it was meant to produce |

**The objectives compete.** The chapter presents three and never sets them
against each other:

- **market share against profitability.** Share is bought with price cuts,
  promotion and range breadth, all of which cost. A business can buy share it
  cannot afford.
- **product range against cost and service.** A wider mix raises warehousing,
  complexity and the training burden on staff who must know the range, which is
  the source's own narrow-range case read as a trade-off.
- **customer service against cost.** Training, rewards, relationship management
  and research are all spending, and the return arrives as repeat purchase later.

**Marketing objectives sit under business objectives.** The chapter says they
should align with overall goals while being more customer-oriented, which makes
market share a marketing objective and profit a business one. That is why growth
in share without profit is a coherent thing to criticise.

**Monitoring and controlling connect marketing to every other function.**
Implementation asks whether the plan is integrated with the rest of the business
and whether all employees know the objectives, which makes the marketing plan an
operations and human resources problem at the point of execution. Most failures
of a marketing plan are implementation failures rather than strategy failures,
and the chapter's list of implementation questions is effectively a list of the
ways it goes wrong.

## Trade-offs and conditions (DERIVED)

- **A measurable objective is not automatically the right objective.** Market
  share is measurable and can be pursued destructively. Measurability makes an
  objective controllable, not correct.
- **Correction takes time the plan may not have.** Monitoring produces
  information after the spending has happened, so a plan can only be corrected
  for its remaining life.
- **Product deletion is resisted for non-commercial reasons.** The chapter notes
  businesses find deletion difficult especially where a product has long been
  successful, which is an inertia argument sitting inside marketing.
- **Promotion cost tracks the life cycle, so an ROI figure is stage-dependent.**
  A launch campaign compared against a mature product's campaign is not a like
  comparison, and the chapter gives the life cycle pattern without drawing that
  conclusion.

## Terminology (SOURCE)

- **marketing objectives** — realistic and measurable goals to be achieved
  through the marketing plan
- **market share** — the business's share of total industry sales for a product
- **product mix** — the total range of products offered
- **product deletion** — eliminating some lines of products
- **customer service** — how well a business meets and exceeds customer
  expectations across its operations
- **customer-oriented** — focused on the customer's needs rather than on making a
  sale
- **implementation** — putting marketing strategies into operation
- **monitoring** — measuring actual performance against planned performance
- **controlling** — comparing planned with actual performance and taking
  corrective action
- **key performance indicator (KPI)** — a forecast level of performance against
  which actual performance is compared
- **financial forecast** — the business's predictions about future costs and
  revenues
- **cost-benefit analysis** — comparing forecast costs against forecast benefits
  to choose between strategies
- **sales analysis** — comparing actual sales with forecast sales
- **market share analysis** — evaluating the business's position against
  competitors
- **marketing return on investment (ROI)** — revenue generated against the cost of
  generating it
- **sales quota** — the sales target set for a territory or salesperson

## Distinctions and confusions

- **SOURCE. Monitoring is not controlling.** Monitoring observes and gathers;
  controlling compares against the KPI and takes corrective action. The chapter
  separates them explicitly.
- **DERIVED. Sales are not market share.** Both can move independently, and the
  difference between them is the whole diagnostic value of market share analysis.
- **DERIVED. An objective is not a strategy.** Increasing market share is an
  objective; cutting price to do it is a strategy. Students state a strategy and
  call it an objective constantly.
- **DERIVED. Effectiveness is not success.** Effectiveness is performance against
  the objective that was set. A business can succeed commercially while its
  marketing plan was ineffective, and the reverse.
- **DERIVED. Customer service is not the people element of the mix.** Service is
  an objective the business is trying to achieve; people, processes and physical
  evidence are strategies used to achieve it.
- **DERIVED. ROI is not profit.** It relates revenue growth to marketing cost and
  ignores every other cost of producing what was sold.

## Instructional implications (USE)

Paragraph shape for an effectiveness question:

1. name the objective and its target, as a number if the case gives one
2. name the strategy used to pursue it
3. state the mechanism connecting them
4. name the indicator that would show whether it worked, and give the result
5. control for the market: was the change caused by the strategy or by conditions
6. judge effectiveness against the target, and say whether the target was the
   right one

Steps 4 and 5 are where mkt-02 answers are won and lost. Most students reach step
3 and then assert success. Step 6 is the difference between "assess" and
"evaluate" being answered properly.

Candidate pathways: price strategy to market share, tested by share analysis;
range expansion to product mix objective, tested by sales analysis per line;
people and processes strategy to customer service objective, tested by retention;
promotion spend to sales growth, tested by marketing ROI with the forecasting
limitation attached.

## Adjacent concepts

The marketing process (situational analysis, SWOT, product life cycle, market
research); target markets and segmentation; the marketing mix and the extended
mix; influences on marketing, especially economic conditions as the confounder
market share analysis is designed to strip out; financial objectives and
profitability management; KPIs in operations and human resources.

## Source gaps

- The three objectives are never set against each other, and one section contains
  both "expand the product mix" as an objective and a successful narrow-range
  case without noticing the contradiction.
- No guidance on choosing objectives, only on measuring them, so a student cannot
  argue that an objective was wrong without supplying the reasoning themselves.
- Effectiveness is defined through three indicators and none is matched to the
  objective it best tests.
- The chapter concedes that revenue forecasts are unreliable and interpretations
  vary, then presents ROI figures without carrying that caveat forward.
- Market share percentages, company R&D spending, sales growth figures and the
  five to one ROI benchmark are quoted without citations a student could use.
  Background for authoring, not evidence.
