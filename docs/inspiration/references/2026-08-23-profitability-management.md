## Definition

**Profitability management** is controlling both sides of profit: the business's
costs and its revenue. It splits into:

- **cost controls** — fixed and variable costs, cost centres, expense
  minimisation
- **revenue controls** — marketing objectives, sales objectives, sales mix,
  pricing policy

Accurate and current financial data is the precondition for any of it, since
every control here depends on knowing a number.

## Explanation

**Fixed and variable costs.** Fixed costs do not depend on the level of activity
and must be paid whatever happens: salaries, depreciation, insurance, lease. They
are not permanently fixed, since new premises change the lease, but once changed
they are fixed again at the new level. Variable costs move directly with the
level of production or activity: materials, labour, energy. They are usually
identifiable against a particular product.

The management task is monitoring both as activity changes, and comparing costs
against budgets, standards and previous periods.

The consequence the source leaves implicit is worth authoring, because it decides
which businesses can survive a downturn: **a business with high fixed costs
cannot shrink its cost base when sales fall.** That is why the fixed and variable
split is on the syllabus at all, and it is the link to break-even.

**Cost centres.** A cost centre is a department or section responsible for a set
of activities, treated as a separate unit so the business can measure what it
spends on that function. It allows costs to be measured, budgeted and controlled
per function, and it helps managers use resources better because they can see how
they are being used. Examples: IT, human resources, accounting, maintenance. A
department can hold several cost centres; in manufacturing, each assembly line
can be one.

The main function of a cost centre is to **track expenses**, and tracking is what
makes control possible. An unattributed cost cannot be managed by anyone, because
nobody owns it.

**Expense minimisation.** Every part of a business creates cost, so management
must examine all activities and decide where cost can be cut. The stated
difficulty is the real one: balancing cost savings against quality. Many
businesses aim to cut cost and pass the saving to customers without significantly
reducing the value of the product. Policies and guidelines encourage staff to
minimise expense, and savings can be substantial where waste and unnecessary
spending are removed.

Cost leadership as a whole business model shows what systematic expense
minimisation looks like when every function participates: simple designs using
low-cost materials and few parts; long-term supplier partnerships with
high-volume orders for economies of scale; packaging engineered so trucks fill
completely, which saves fuel, transport and storage; sites outside city centres
where land is cheaper; and mass production of a limited range. The point for
students is that no single one of those is decisive. Cost leadership is the
accumulation, and it is a design decision before it is a finance one.

**Revenue controls.** Revenue is income from the main activity: sales, or fees
and commission for a service business. Raising profit can be done by raising
revenue, and doing that deliberately requires clear policies on marketing
objectives.

- **Sales objectives** must be set at a level that covers both fixed and variable
  costs and leaves a profit. **Cost-volume-profit analysis** determines the
  revenue needed to break even and predicts the effect on profit of changes in
  activity, price or cost. Budgets and cost-volume-profit analysis are the tools
  of revenue control.
- **Sales mix.** Changing the mix changes revenue. The control is keeping a clear
  focus on the customer base most revenue depends on before diversifying,
  extending ranges, or discontinuing lines, and researching the likely effect
  before deciding.
- **Pricing policy.** Overpricing fails to attract buyers; underpricing may raise
  sales volume and still produce cash shortfalls and low profit. Pricing is
  influenced by the cost of producing the good or service (materials, labour,
  overheads), competitors' prices, short and long-term goals (a business
  pursuing market share over five years may cut price), the image or quality
  level customers associate with the product, and government policy.

The pricing problem is genuinely two-sided and that is what makes it examinable:
both errors reduce profit, by different routes.

**When costs rise and price cannot.** The source's grocery case shows the third
option students never name. A manufacturer facing rising input, labour and energy
costs, in a market where consumers will switch to imported or private-label
alternatives, has three moves: raise price, reduce product size, or cut the range
produced. Reducing quantity at an unchanged price is a price rise expressed
through the product rather than the ticket. Recognising that as a pricing
decision is a strong observation for an essay.

## Relationships to other concepts

Profitability management is a **cause** acting on the financial objectives, most
directly on **profitability**, and its distinguishing feature is that it is the
one financial strategy that runs *through* the other functions rather than
alongside them. Cost control happens in operations. Revenue control happens in
marketing. Finance sets the targets and measures the result.

| decision | objective raised | objective paid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| expense minimisation | profitability, efficiency | quality, and with it the customer's perception of value |
| cost centres | efficiency, through visibility of where money goes | a small administrative cost, and a risk of local optimisation at the whole business's expense |
| cutting price to gain share | growth, long term | profitability, short term; this is the growth against profit conflict in its most common form |
| raising price | profitability per unit | growth and volume, if customers switch |
| raising volume through low price | growth, revenue | liquidity, since higher sales can still produce cash shortfalls |

That last row is the one worth teaching, because it is the least intuitive:
underpricing may bring higher sales and still produce a cash shortfall. Revenue
is not cash and volume is not profit.

The connection to marketing is not decoration. Sales objectives, sales mix and
pricing policy are marketing decisions listed in the finance syllabus, which is
the clearest statement in the course that the functions are interdependent. A
fin-01 paragraph that runs from a pricing decision to a profitability outcome is
using that interdependence exactly as intended.

## Terminology

- **profitability management** — control of both costs and revenue
- **fixed cost** — a cost that does not change with the level of activity
- **variable cost** — a cost that moves directly with the level of activity
- **cost centre** — a department or section to which costs can be directly
  attributed and separately measured
- **expense minimisation** — examining all activities to find where cost can be
  cut without unacceptable loss of value
- **revenue** — income earned from the main activity of the business
- **revenue controls** — policies on sales objectives, sales mix and pricing that
  govern income
- **sales mix** — the combination of products or lines a business sells
- **pricing policy** — the rules by which prices are set
- **cost-volume-profit analysis** — the calculation of the revenue needed to
  break even and the effect on profit of changes in activity, price or cost
- **break-even** — the level of revenue at which fixed and variable costs are
  exactly covered
- **cost leadership** — competing by being the lowest-cost producer
- **overheads** — costs not directly attributable to producing a specific unit

## Distinctions and confusions

- **Revenue is not profit.** Revenue is income before costs. A business can
  raise revenue and lower profit by doing it through discounting.
- **Profit is not cash.** Higher sales can coincide with a cash shortfall. This
  is the same distinction that drives cash flow management, arriving from the
  revenue side.
- **Fixed costs are not permanent.** They do not vary with activity. They can be
  changed by a decision, and then they are fixed at a new level.
- **Cost centre is not profit centre.** A cost centre is measured on what it
  spends. It earns no revenue of its own, which is why it is judged on cost
  rather than on return.
- **Expense minimisation is not cost cutting without limit.** The stated
  constraint is quality and the customer's perception of value. An answer that
  cuts costs without naming that constraint has missed the difficulty.
- **Cheaper is not always more profitable.** Underpricing raises volume and can
  still fail on both profit and cash.
- **Reducing product size is a price change.** Same money, less product.

## Worked example, structurally

1. name the cost or revenue lever precisely: which cost, which price, which line
   of the sales mix
2. name the objective it targets, in the objective's own vocabulary
3. state the mechanism, and put a direction on it: which number rises, which
   falls, and why
4. name what it costs: quality, volume, a customer segment, or cash timing
5. judge whether it suited this business, given whether its costs are mostly
   fixed or mostly variable

Step 5 gives an answer something specific to say. A business with heavy fixed
costs must protect volume, so cutting price to hold volume can be rational. A
business whose costs are mostly variable can shrink with demand and has less to
fear from a fall in volume.

## Adjacent concepts

Financial objectives, especially profitability, efficiency and growth;
break-even analysis and cost-volume-profit; budgets; cash flow and working
capital management; pricing methods and the marketing mix; cost leadership as a
business strategy; operations cost control and economies of scale.

## What the source does not give us

- No statement of the consequence of the fixed and variable split, which is that
  high fixed costs make a business fragile when volume falls. The distinction is
  taught and its point is left out.
- No link from cost or revenue controls to any named financial objective, and no
  treatment of profitability against liquidity, though the underpricing example
  gestures at it.
- Expense minimisation names quality as the constraint and gives no method for
  deciding how far to go.
- Cost centres are described as a tracking mechanism with no downside, and no
  mention of a department optimising its own numbers at the expense of the
  business.
- Case material is journalism and company reporting: shrinking package sizes,
  job loss figures and profit percentages are all quoted second hand. Background
  for authoring, not student evidence.
