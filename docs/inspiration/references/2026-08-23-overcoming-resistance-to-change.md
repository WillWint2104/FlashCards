## Definition

Operations strategies mostly describe something a business does. This one
describes something that stops it. **Resistance to change** is what a business
must overcome before any other operations strategy can be implemented, and it
comes from two sources:

- **financial costs** — the money that has to be spent before the change works
- **inertia** — psychological resistance to change

Change itself is either external (legislation, the economy, social expectations,
new technology) or internal (staff initiative, available technology, innovation
in goods or services). External change is the kind a business must adapt to, and
adaptation may require significant internal realignment.

## Explanation

The reason change is resisted is worth stating in the source's own economic
terms, because it is a better explanation than "people don't like change":
change causes uncertainty, uncertainty is risk, and most people are risk averse.
That is true of the owner deciding whether to spend, and of the employee
deciding whether to support the decision. Both are avoiding a risk.

The first step in overcoming resistance is understanding why it exists in this
particular business. Only then can a strategy address it.

**The financial costs.**

*Purchasing new equipment.* Machinery and technology are a capital cost, usually
high, recouped through use and depreciation. What the spend buys is worth
naming, because these are performance objectives in disguise: better processing
flexibility and speed with shorter lead times, more consistency, higher quality
in processing, and less waste and loss from equipment failure. Before buying,
managers usually compare purchase against leasing. Leasing avoids the large
up-front payment and costs more as an ongoing operating expense. That is a
liquidity decision inside an operations one.

*Redundancy payments.* Redundancy is the loss of work because a job no longer
exists, typically from technological change, restructure, or a merger. The payout
depends on length of service (with a legislated minimum), the worker's wage or
salary, accrued unused leave including annual and long service leave, and any
outstanding wages. Because those accumulate, redundancy costs are large and
largest exactly when the change is largest: when capital replaces labour, which
is **capital-labour substitution**.

*Retraining.* Comes from two directions: a restructure changes job roles, or new
technology requires skills nobody currently has. Either way, the benefit of the
new equipment cannot be realised without spending on training and development.
This is the sentence that makes retraining a cost of change rather than an HR
activity.

*Reorganising plant layout.* Plant is the facility the machinery sits in, and
its layout is normally built around the goods being made and the processes
making them. Re-engineering the process means rebuilding the layout, and the
costs are not only construction. There is downtime while transferring from old
machinery to new, testing the new capacity, and lost productivity while staff
learn the changed processes. Those three are the costs students never think of,
and they are often larger than the equipment.

**Inertia.** Psychological resistance to change: uncertainty, or fear of the
unknown, when change is imminent. It intensifies where people believe their job
is threatened, fear losing career opportunity, or find new equipment
intimidating. Inertia is not irrational; it is a rational response to a personal
risk the business is asking someone else to bear.

The management response the source implies but states only through a quoted
consultant is communication: whether the business has given as clear a picture
as it can of what is changing, when, how and why. That is worth authoring as the
concrete answer to "how is inertia overcome", because otherwise students have a
problem with no solution.

## Relationships to other concepts

This topic is the **precondition** of every other operations strategy, and that
is the framing worth teaching. A business cannot adopt JIT, install leading edge
technology, restructure a supply chain or roll out Six Sigma without incurring
these costs and meeting this resistance. So the answer to "why doesn't every
business adopt the best strategy" is here.

That makes it the natural source of the **limitation** step in an ops-01
paragraph. A paragraph that says a strategy improved an objective, then says what
it cost to get there and why some businesses cannot pay it, has used this topic
correctly without writing a separate paragraph about it.

The financial costs also map straight onto objectives:

- redundancy and retraining are the cost of a **flexibility** gain
- equipment purchase is the cost of **speed**, **quality** and **consistency**
- downtime during a layout change is a temporary loss of **dependability** and
  **speed**, accepted for a permanent gain

Inertia connects to human resources at almost every point: participation in
decision making, communication, training as a way of removing the fear rather
than only the skill gap, and redundancy handled well or badly. A student can use
this topic to link operations and HRM legitimately, which examiners reward.

## Terminology

- **resistance to change** — opposition to change within a business, financial
  or psychological
- **inertia** — psychological resistance to change; fear of the unknown
- **capital cost** — the up-front cost of equipment, recouped through use and
  depreciation
- **leasing** — paying to use equipment over time instead of buying it; no large
  up-front cost, higher ongoing cost
- **redundancy** — loss of a job because the job no longer exists
- **redundancy payout** — the payment owed, based on service length, wage,
  accrued leave and outstanding wages
- **capital-labour substitution** — replacing labour with machinery and
  technology
- **retraining** — equipping employees with the skills a changed role or new
  technology requires
- **plant** — the facility where machinery is arranged
- **plant layout** — how that machinery is arranged, normally organised around
  the process
- **downtime** — production time lost while changing over

## Distinctions and confusions

- **Resistance is not only from employees.** Managers and owners resist too, and
  their resistance is financial. Students write this topic as though it were
  entirely about staff attitude, which loses half of it.
- **Inertia is not laziness.** It is a rational response to personal risk. Framing
  it as an attitude problem produces answers that propose no workable solution.
- **Redundancy is not dismissal.** Redundancy means the job ceased to exist. It
  carries legislated entitlements that dismissal for cause does not.
- **Retraining is not a benefit of change.** It is a cost of it. It appears in
  the syllabus under financial costs, and treating it as an HR benefit misreads
  the topic.
- **The cost of a plant layout change is not the construction.** It is mostly
  downtime and lost productivity while people learn. That is where the money
  actually goes.
- **Overcoming resistance is a strategy, not a preamble.** It is examinable in
  its own right, and it is the answer to why a good strategy is not adopted.

## Worked example, structurally

1. name the change the business is attempting and the operations strategy behind
   it
2. name the resistance, and say which of the two kinds it is
3. state the mechanism of the resistance: what specific cost, or what specific
   fear, and whose
4. name what the business did about it
5. judge whether the change delivered enough to justify what overcoming the
   resistance cost

Step 3 is the difference between a real answer and a list of headings. "Staff
resisted" is not an answer; "staff whose skills were specific to the old process
faced redundancy rather than redeployment, so their resistance was to job loss
rather than to the technology" is.

## Adjacent concepts

Every other operations strategy, since this is the cost of adopting any of them;
technology, especially leading edge; human resource management, particularly
training and development, participation and redundancy; the legal influence on
operations through redundancy entitlements; liquidity and the lease against buy
decision; performance objectives as what the spending is meant to buy.

## What the source does not give us

- Almost nothing on *overcoming* the resistance, despite that being the name of
  the topic. The costs and the causes are set out; the strategies for managing
  them appear once, in a quoted consultant's question about communication.
- No treatment of participation, consultation, phased implementation, incentives
  or leadership, all of which are standard answers and none of which is here.
- No link from these costs to the performance objectives the spending is meant to
  buy, though both are in the same chapter.
- The redundancy example given is a company announcement of job losses, with no
  cost attached, so it demonstrates that redundancies happen rather than what
  they cost.
