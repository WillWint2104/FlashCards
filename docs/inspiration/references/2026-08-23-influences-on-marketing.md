## Concepts (SOURCE)

Four factors influence customer choice:

| factor | definition |
| --- | --- |
| **psychological** | influences within an individual that affect buying behaviour |
| **sociocultural** | forces exerted by other people and groups |
| **economic** | forces affecting a business's capacity to compete and a customer's willingness and ability to spend |
| **government** | policies and laws that directly or indirectly affect business activity and spending habits |

Alongside them sit **consumer laws**, which constrain what marketing may do, and
**ethical influences**, which constrain what it should do.

## Explanation (SOURCE)

**Psychological, five of them.**

- **Perception** — how people select, organise and interpret information to
  create meaning. People act on their perception of reality rather than on
  reality, so marketing works to create a favourable perception, often an image
  such as trendy, luxurious or rebellious that the product need not itself
  possess.
- **Motives** — a person's reason for doing something: comfort, health, safety,
  ambition, taste, pleasure, fear, amusement, cleanliness, the approval of
  others. Advertising works on motives directly.
- **Attitudes** — overall feeling about an object or activity. Negative
  attitudes to a business or its products often force a change of strategy.
- **Personality and self-image** — the behaviours and characteristics making up
  a person, and how they view themselves. Self-image is a major determinant of
  what people buy, because purchases reinforce the image. This is why celebrity
  endorsement works: buyers want their self-image to reflect people they regard
  as important.
- **Learning** — changes in behaviour caused by information and experience, both
  direct and indirect. Behaviour with a satisfying outcome tends to be repeated,
  which is habit formation, and habit is closely linked to **brand loyalty**.

**Sociocultural, four of them.**

- **Social class** (socioeconomic status) — relative rank based on education,
  occupation and income. It influences the type, quality and quantity bought.
- **Culture and subculture** — the learned values, beliefs, behaviours and
  traditions shared by a society, determining what people wear, eat and how they
  live. A shift toward interest in healthier eating produces low-fat, sugar-free
  and fibre-enriched ranges.
- **Family and roles** — the roles people occupy shape what they buy and decide.
  Teenagers are documented as a household's chief influencer across clothes,
  shoes, music, books, food, household goods and travel, and **pester power**
  names children's ability to secure a purchase by repeated asking.
- **Reference (peer) group** — people a person identifies with closely enough to
  adopt their attitudes, values and beliefs.

**Economic.** Activity fluctuates between boom and recession. In a **boom**,
unemployment is low, incomes rise, businesses and customers are optimistic,
production lines and promotional effort expand, and sales respond to all forms of
promotion. In a **recession**, unemployment rises, incomes fall, and customers
and businesses become pessimistic and cut spending.

**Government.** Two distinct mechanisms, and the source separates them.
*Economic policy* expands or contracts activity and so shifts spending
indirectly; the chapter's worked instance is a set of pandemic income-support
and wage-subsidy payments that held employment and consumer spending up.
*Regulation* is more direct and immediate: statutes and regulatory bodies whose
breach carries financial penalties.

**Consumer laws.** The **Australian Consumer Law**, introduced in 2011, replaced
seventeen national, state and territory laws with one set of rules applying to
all Australian consumers and businesses wherever they operate or shop. It is
applied and implemented by the **Competition and Consumer Act 2010** (Cwlth) as a
Commonwealth law and by each state and territory's own application Act, enforced
by the ACCC and by state and territory consumer agencies, with mirror
protections in the ASIC Act enforced by ASIC. Older instruments named in the
chapter include the Sale of Goods Act 1923 (NSW) and the Fair Trading Act 1987
(NSW).

Four areas the syllabus names:

- **Deceptive and misleading advertising.** Any representation, direct or
  implied, must not be untrue or false and must not be likely to mislead the
  consumers it targets. Named methods: **bait advertising** (advertising at
  reduced prices goods not available or available only in small quantity, then
  switching customers to higher priced items); **fine print** that contradicts
  the overall message; **comparative advertising** where the comparison is
  inaccurate or inappropriate; and **environmental claims** that cannot be
  substantiated.
- **Price discrimination.** Charging different prices for the same product based
  on what the seller believes a customer will accept. The chapter's illustration
  is algorithmic surge and route-based pricing that predicts a user's maximum
  from location, time, traffic and history.
- **Implied conditions** — the unspoken, unwritten terms of a contract, expressed
  through **consumer guarantees**. Goods must be of **acceptable quality**: safe,
  lasting, without faults, acceptable in appearance, and doing what someone would
  normally expect, judged against the type of product and its cost. They must
  match descriptions given by a salesperson, on packaging or in advertising;
  match any sample or demonstration model; be fit for the purpose the business
  stated and any purpose the buyer made known; carry no hidden debts; meet extra
  promises such as lifetime guarantees; and have spare parts and repair
  available for a reasonable time. Services must be delivered with acceptable
  care and skill, be fit for the agreed purpose, and be delivered within a
  reasonable time. A remedy is claimed from the retailer, who cannot redirect the
  consumer to the manufacturer.
- **Warranties** — a promise to correct defects, that is to repair or replace
  faulty goods or remedy services. Terms and conditions must be stated clearly
  and simply, and false or misleading statements about a warranty's existence or
  exclusions are prohibited.

**Ethical influences.** Criticisms named include the **creation of needs**
through materialism, where promotional strategy persuades customers to buy
whatever the business wants to sell, and **stereotyping**, most commonly of
gender, where portrayals do not represent reality. On **truth and accuracy**,
the chapter notes that exaggeration is close to the norm, that terms like
"special", "great value", "low fat" and "light" can be read many ways, and that
the main unethical practices are concealed facts, exaggerated claims, vague
statements and invasion of privacy. Consumers who discover an untruth may feel
cheated, stop buying, and complain to a regulator, with lasting reputational
damage.

## Load-bearing derived claims (DERIVED)

```
SOURCE  Deceptive and misleading advertising is illegal under the Competition and
        Consumer Act. Separately, the ethics section says exaggeration is close to the
        rule of thumb and that words like "special" and "great value" can be read many
        ways.
DERIVED Those two passages describe the same territory from opposite sides. The law
        catches representations likely to mislead; ethics covers what is technically
        defensible and still designed to be misread. The gap between them is where
        most real marketing decisions sit.
USE     The spine of mkt-03 and of any "ethical or legal" question: legal compliance
        is the floor, and a student who can name a practice that is lawful and still
        criticised has answered the harder half.
```

```
SOURCE  Psychological factors operate internally but are stated to be very much
        affected by sociocultural forces outside the individual.
DERIVED The four influences are not four independent inputs. Sociocultural forces
        shape the psychological ones: a reference group supplies the attitudes, a
        culture supplies the motives, social class shapes the self-image a purchase
        reinforces. Economic conditions then set whether that preference can be acted
        on, and government sets whether it may be.
USE     Turns a four-item list into a layered account, so a student can explain why a
        preference did not convert into a purchase, which is what "to what extent do
        influences determine success" is asking.
```

```
SOURCE  Government appears twice with different mechanisms: economic policy shifting
        spending, and regulation carrying penalties.
DERIVED Those work on different timescales and in opposite directions for the
        marketer. Policy changes how much customers can spend and is an opportunity;
        regulation changes what may be offered and is a constraint. Treating
        "government" as one influence loses that.
USE     A precision check. An answer naming which of the two it means is immediately
        more specific than one that says government affects marketing.
```

```
SOURCE  Consumer guarantees give rights to a remedy, and the chapter records court
        penalties against businesses that told consumers rights had expired or that
        fares were not refundable.
DERIVED Consumer law binds the marketing message as tightly as the product: a
        statement about a customer's rights is itself a representation and can be the
        breach, even where the product was fine.
USE     Lets a student write about consumer law without needing a faulty product,
        which is where most attempts get stuck.
```

## Relationships and mechanisms (DERIVED unless marked)

Influences are the **conditions** marketing works inside. Strategies are the
response. That separation is what mkt-03 tests, and the most common failure is
answering an influence question with a list of strategies.

How each influence reaches a marketing decision:

| influence | what it changes | the strategy that answers it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| psychological | what the customer wants the product to mean | promotion, branding, positioning |
| sociocultural | what is acceptable, expected, or aspired to | product range, promotion, segmentation variables |
| economic | whether the customer can act on the preference | pricing, value ranges, promotional intensity |
| government policy | how much money is in customers' hands | timing and scale of campaigns |
| government regulation | what may lawfully be claimed or charged | advertising content, pricing practice, warranty terms |
| ethical expectation | what will be tolerated even where lawful | what the business declines to do |

**The influences do not weigh equally, and which dominates changes with
conditions.** In a boom, psychological and sociocultural influences do most of
the work, because ability to pay is not the binding constraint. In a recession,
economic influence dominates and preference becomes secondary, which is why value
ranges appear and premium lines contract. A student who says which influence was
binding for this business at this time has produced an argument rather than a
survey.

**Regulation is not only a constraint, it is a competitive leveller.** A rule
applying to every business in a market removes a practice from the set of
available advantages. The business harmed most by a ban on an unsubstantiated
claim is the one that was relying on it.

**Ethical behaviour has a marketing return and it is conditional.** The stated
mechanism is that consumers who discover an untruth stop buying and complain, so
the return depends on discovery. Where discovery is unlikely, the ethical
constraint has to be internal, which is exactly why a code of ethics exists as a
separate instrument from the law.

## Trade-offs and conditions (DERIVED)

- **Persuasion against trust.** Every psychological technique that makes a
  product more desirable than it is buys short-term response with the risk of
  the customer feeling cheated later. The chapter supplies both halves and never
  joins them.
- **Segmentation against stereotyping.** Marketing to a group requires
  generalising about it. The criticism of stereotyping and the practice of
  demographic segmentation are the same activity judged by different standards,
  and the line between them is whether the portrayal is used to reach people or
  to define them.
- **Price discrimination against fairness.** Charging what each customer will
  bear is efficient and is also the practice consumer law scrutinises most, so
  the constraint here is legal, ethical and reputational at once.
- **Compliance cost against reach.** Meeting every disclosure requirement makes
  advertising longer, denser and less persuasive. Fine print exists because of
  that pressure, and the law then regulates the fine print.

## Terminology (SOURCE)

- **psychological factors** — influences within an individual affecting buying
  behaviour
- **perception** — selecting, organising and interpreting information to create
  meaning
- **motive** — an individual's reason for doing something
- **attitude** — a person's overall feeling about an object or activity
- **personality** — the behaviours and characteristics making up an individual
- **self-image** — how a person views themselves
- **learning** — changes in behaviour caused by information and experience
- **brand loyalty** — a favourable attitude to one brand producing repeat sales
- **sociocultural influences** — forces exerted by other people and groups
- **social class / socioeconomic status** — relative rank based on education,
  income or occupation
- **culture** — learned values, beliefs, behaviours and traditions shared by a
  society
- **reference / peer group** — people whose attitudes, values and beliefs a
  person adopts
- **pester power** — children's ability to secure a purchase by repeated asking
- **boom** — low unemployment, rising incomes, optimism, rising spending
- **recession** — high unemployment, falling incomes, pessimism, falling spending
- **Australian Consumer Law (ACL)** — the single national consumer law from 2011
- **Competition and Consumer Act 2010 (Cwlth)** — the Act applying and
  implementing the ACL federally
- **bait advertising** — advertising goods at low prices that are unavailable or
  very limited, then switching customers to dearer items
- **comparative advertising** — comparing products, misleading where the
  comparison is inaccurate
- **price discrimination** — charging different prices for the same product based
  on what the seller believes each customer will accept
- **implied conditions** — the unspoken, unwritten terms of a contract
- **consumer guarantees** — the rights to a remedy where goods or services fail
  the ACL standards
- **acceptable quality** — safe, lasting, faultless, acceptable in appearance and
  doing what would normally be expected for that product and price
- **warranty** — a promise to correct defects by repair or replacement
- **unconscionable conduct** — a business practice that is not reasonable and is
  often illegal
- **materialism** — a desire to constantly acquire possessions
- **product placement** — including advertising within entertainment
- **sugging** — selling under the guise of research

## Distinctions and confusions

- **SOURCE. Perception is not reality.** The chapter states that people act on
  perception rather than reality, and that a product may not have the qualities
  its image conveys.
- **DERIVED. An influence is not a strategy.** Influences are conditions the
  business responds to. Answering mkt-03 with a list of marketing strategies is
  the most common failure on it.
- **DERIVED. A warranty is not a consumer guarantee.** Consumer guarantees are
  statutory and automatic. A warranty is a promise the business chooses to make,
  and it cannot reduce the guarantees.
- **DERIVED. Implied conditions are not written terms.** They apply whether or
  not anyone wrote them down, which is why a business cannot advertise its way
  out of them.
- **DERIVED. Price discrimination is not price variation.** Charging less at a
  quiet hour is a pricing strategy. Charging a specific customer more because an
  algorithm predicts they will pay it is discrimination in this sense.
- **DERIVED. Psychological is not sociocultural.** Inside the person against
  exerted by others. Self-image is psychological; the peer group that shapes it
  is sociocultural, and the two appear in the same sentence in most examples.
- **DERIVED. Economic influence is not price.** Price is a strategy. Economic
  influence is the condition, boom or recession, that makes a price work or fail.

## Instructional implications (USE)

Paragraph shape for an influence question:

1. name the influence and its category, precisely
2. state what it changed about the customer or the market, not about the business
3. name the marketing response it produced
4. state the mechanism connecting the two
5. say how far the influence determined the outcome, and name a second influence
   that cut across it

Step 5 is what a "to what extent" command word is asking for, and it is only
answerable if the student knows the influences interact rather than sit in four
boxes. Step 2 is the discipline that keeps an influence answer from becoming a
strategy answer.

Candidate pathways: economic conditions to pricing and value ranges;
sociocultural change to product range; regulation to advertising content;
psychological self-image to promotion and endorsement; ethical criticism to
voluntary change beyond compliance.

## Source gaps

- The four influences are presented as a list of four, with one sentence
  conceding that psychological factors are affected by sociocultural ones. The
  interaction between them, and the question of which dominates when, is not
  addressed.
- Government appears as one influence carrying two unrelated mechanisms, and the
  chapter never separates policy from regulation.
- Law and ethics are given consecutive sections with no account of the space
  between them, which is where the interesting cases are.
- No treatment of what an influence costs a business to respond to, so every
  response reads as free.
- Case material (court penalties, survey findings on teen influence and pester
  power, pandemic payment amounts) is real but quoted second hand without
  citations usable by a student. Background for authoring, not evidence.
