## Concepts (SOURCE)

**Supply chain management** is integrating and managing the flow of supplies
across inputs, transformation and outputs so the business meets customer needs.
It covers both the supply side (sourcing) and the delivery side (logistics and
distribution), because the chain is shaped by what is sold and what comes back.

Three parts: **logistics**, **e-commerce**, **global sourcing**.

A useful way in, and the source's own: start at the finished good and trace
backwards through every process that added value, all the way to raw inputs.
Doing that exposes the whole chain, including the suppliers, energy, labour and
distribution a forward-looking view leaves out. It is a systems view, and it is
what a strategy has to be built on.

## Explanation (SOURCE)

**Logistics** is distribution plus everything that supports it: transport and
the choice of mode, storage, warehousing and distribution centres, materials
handling and packaging.

*Transport mode* is a genuine choice with a structure to it. Speed, distance,
cost and capacity trade against each other and no mode wins on all four. A
courier is fast, cheap and carries almost nothing. Air is fast over any distance
and very expensive. Sea and rail are slow, cheap, and carry volumes nothing else
can. Some goods have no choice at all, because bulk commodities can only move by
ship or rail. So the mode is set by the nature of the good and the cost the
business can carry, and that is the sentence a student needs rather than the
table.

*Storage* holds stock securely until it is needed. It is necessary where there
are many outlets, or where demand varies enough that supply has to be
responsive. Some goods force it: cold storage extends shelf life for perishables.

*Warehousing* is a facility for storage, protection and later distribution, and
it carries costs students can list: premises, insurance and security, stacking
and moving, excess or redundant stock, shrinkage and theft, and damage from
incorrect storage.

*Distribution centres* are the distinction worth making. A DC is not a warehouse
for long-term storage. It is positioned to minimise the time it takes to get
stock to retail outlets, often beside a transport hub. The decision is explicitly
a trade: the cost of running the centre against the time saved in logistics. Big
retailers site them to cut lead times and avoid stock-outs.

*Materials handling and packaging* is where regulation bites. Fragile goods need
protective packaging; dangerous goods have legally mandated storage, handling and
labelling. This is a small part of the syllabus that links directly to the legal
influence on operations.

**E-commerce** is buying and selling over the internet, and it changes both ends
of the chain.

- **E-procurement / B2B.** Online supply management gives suppliers direct
  visibility of the buyer's stock level, so replacement is triggered when stock
  falls to a set point, without a formal order. That is the mechanism, and it is
  the one students should be able to state: the supplier can see the buyer's
  level and acts on it.
- **B2C.** Selling directly to consumers online, or letting a specialist
  intermediary site sell on the business's behalf. It multiplies ordering
  channels, so stock levels have to be accurate and shared frequently, or the
  business promises customers stock it does not have.

E-commerce also reshapes physical distribution, because a consumer ordering
online expects delivery that is fast, reliable and secure, which is a different
logistics problem from restocking a shop.

**Global sourcing** is acquiring supplies or services without being constrained
by location: buying from whichever supplier best meets the requirement,
wherever it is.

Before that, plain **sourcing** (procurement, purchasing) is choosing suppliers
against four factors:

1. consumer demand, which sets the volume of inputs needed
2. the quality of inputs required, which follows from the quality of output the
   business intends to sell
3. the supplier's flexibility and timeliness when demand changes
4. cost, compared against other suppliers offering similar quality

Global sourcing brings cost and expertise advantages and access to technology
and resources unavailable locally. Against that: parts of the operations process
may have to relocate, logistics, storage and distribution cost more, regulatory
conditions differ between countries, and the whole operation becomes more complex
to manage as the number of source locations grows.

The most useful thing the source contains on this is inside a case study about
supply shortage: **local supply chains are much more responsive.** Domestic
product returns to shelves in days or weeks; imported product needs a wait
measured in months. Lean, low-inventory chains work beautifully while demand is
predictable and fail visibly the moment it is not. That is the trade-off global
sourcing makes, stated as a real consequence rather than a bullet.

## Relationships and mechanisms (DERIVED unless marked)

Supply chain management is a **cause** acting on performance objectives, and its
distinctive feature is that most of the levers are *outside the business*.
Inventory and quality are things a manager controls. A supplier's reliability is
a thing a manager can only choose, contract for and monitor.

| decision | objective raised | objective paid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| distribution centre near outlets | speed, dependability | cost, since the centre is held capital |
| slower, cheaper transport mode | cost | speed |
| global sourcing on cost | cost | dependability, flexibility, and responsiveness to a demand spike |
| local sourcing | dependability, flexibility, speed of recovery | cost |
| e-procurement with supplier visibility | dependability, speed, lower holding cost | control, since the supplier now drives replenishment |

Supply chain management is the strategy that makes **JIT possible or impossible**.
JIT depends on deliveries arriving reliably at an appointed time, and whether
that is achievable is a supply chain fact, not an inventory one. A student
arguing for JIT without addressing supplier reliability has not finished the
argument.

It also links out of operations in three directions: to marketing, since place
and distribution are the same physical decisions seen from the customer's side;
to finance, since warehousing and distribution centres are capital and holding
cost; and to the legal and environmental influences on operations, through
dangerous goods regulation and the emissions of long transport chains.

## Terminology (SOURCE)

- **supply chain management (SCM)** — integrating and managing the flow of
  supplies across inputs, transformation and outputs
- **logistics** — distribution, and the transport, storage, warehousing,
  materials handling and packaging that support it
- **distribution** — the ways of getting goods or services to the customer
- **storage** — holding stock securely until it is required
- **warehousing** — using a facility for storage, protection and later
  distribution
- **distribution centre (DC)** — a facility sited for short-term holding and
  fast onward supply, not long-term storage
- **materials handling** — the standards and methods for moving and storing
  goods safely
- **e-commerce** — buying and selling goods and services over the internet
- **e-procurement** — online systems that manage supply and give suppliers
  direct access to the buyer's stock levels
- **B2B (business to business)** — direct supplier access to a buyer's
  requirements
- **B2C (business to consumer)** — selling to consumers over the internet
- **sourcing / procurement / purchasing** — buying inputs for the transformation
  process
- **global sourcing** — sourcing without being constrained by location
- **stock-out** — running out of an item a customer wants to buy
- **lead time** — the interval between order and delivery

## Distinctions and confusions

- **A distribution centre is not a warehouse.** A warehouse holds stock; a DC
  moves it. The DC exists to cut lead time, and it is justified by time saved,
  not by space provided.
- **Logistics is not distribution.** Distribution is one part of logistics.
  Students use the words as synonyms and then cannot say what else logistics
  covers.
- **Global sourcing is not offshoring.** Global sourcing buys inputs from
  overseas suppliers. Offshoring moves the business's own operations overseas.
  Global sourcing may *lead* to relocation, which is listed as one of its
  challenges, but they are different decisions.
- **Global sourcing is not outsourcing.** Outsourcing hands a function to
  another business, which may be domestic. Global sourcing is about where, not
  about who owns the activity.
- **E-commerce is not just online selling.** B2C is the visible half. E-procurement
  on the supply side is the half that actually changes the operations process.
- **Cheaper is not cheaper overall.** A lower input price bought globally is
  offset by higher logistics, storage, distribution, compliance and complexity
  costs. An answer that compares only unit prices has not compared the options.

## Instructional implications (USE)

1. name the supply chain decision precisely: which mode, which supplier
   location, which facility, which ordering system
2. name the performance objective it is chosen to serve
3. state the mechanism, and keep it physical: what actually moves faster,
   arrives more reliably, or costs less, and why
4. name the objective it costs and the risk it accepts, including risks held by
   another business
5. say what about this business's market makes the trade worth it

Step 4 is where supply chain answers stand apart, because the risk usually sits
with someone the business does not control. A student who names that has
understood why supply chain management is a strategy rather than an
administrative task.
