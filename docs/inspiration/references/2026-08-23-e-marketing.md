## Concepts (SOURCE)

E-marketing is the practice of using the internet to carry out marketing
activities. It is a strategy, not a channel: the decision is not "should we have
a website" but which marketing work the business moves online and why.

## Explanation (SOURCE)

The source lists six technologies. They are worth keeping distinct because each
one does a different marketing job, and a student who treats them as
interchangeable cannot explain why a business picked one.

| technology | what it actually does |
| --- | --- |
| web pages | the business's owned information surface; the home page is the entry point and the only element the business fully controls |
| podcasts | rented access to a narrow, self-selected audience; useful precisely because the audience has already declared an interest |
| electronic messages (email, SMS) | direct contact with people the business already has a relationship with; SMS arrives without the recipient doing anything, which is its whole advantage over email |
| blogs | reputation and expertise, plus a two-way channel for feedback; informal by nature, so it puts a human face on the business |
| location-based marketing | targeting by where the customer is right now, so an offer can meet a daily habit |
| social media advertising | cheap reach with measurable exposure, and customers who redistribute the message themselves |

Two properties separate e-marketing from traditional promotion, and both are
worth teaching explicitly.

**It is measurable.** Reach (how many people saw the message) and frequency (how
many times each saw it) can be counted rather than estimated. That turns
marketing spend into something a business can evaluate against a KPI.

**Control is traded away for participation.** The central disadvantage of social
media advertising is that the business does not control what customers say about
it. But the same property is the advantage: customers who share, review and
respond distribute the message for free. A business cannot take the reach without
taking the loss of control, and saying so is what turns a description into an
evaluation.

The source also documents a gap that is useful as a strategic argument: most
small Australian businesses have no website and only about half have a social
media presence, in a market where the large majority of consumers shop online.
The stated risk is that customers wanting online convenience buy from overseas
retailers and bypass local businesses entirely.

## Relationships and mechanisms (DERIVED unless marked)

`mkt-01` asks how the target market affects e-marketing, so again the causal
direction is the thing to author:

| target market characteristic | what it forces |
| --- | --- |
| a narrow interest group, hard to reach by mass media | podcasts or a specialist social platform, where the audience has already self-selected |
| an existing customer base worth retaining | electronic messages, because they reach people who already opted in |
| customers who research before buying | web pages and blogs, because the job is depth of information, not exposure |
| customers who decide in the moment, near a location | location-based marketing, which only works when the purchase is proximate and impulsive |
| a young, socially networked segment | social media advertising, and acceptance of the loss of control that comes with it |
| a segment defined by price sensitivity | the cheapest channels with measurable reach, which is what makes SMA the default for a start-up |

E-marketing also acts on the other three extra Ps rather than sitting beside
them:

- it **becomes** the process when the transaction happens online
- it **becomes** the physical evidence when the site is the only premises the
  customer sees
- it **replaces or reshapes** the people element, since the interaction moves to
  messages answered outside opening hours

That interlock is the strongest available answer to "how do these four elements
hold together for one target market", and the source never states it.

## Terminology (SOURCE)

- **e-marketing (electronic marketing)** — using the internet to perform
  marketing activities. Internet marketing, web marketing and online marketing
  are used interchangeably for the same thing.
- **podcasting** — distributing digital audio or video files over the internet,
  usually to subscribers
- **blog** — an online journal that readers can add to
- **commercial electronic message** — any electronic message that offers,
  advertises or promotes a business, good or service
- **location-based marketing** — delivering targeted messages based on where the
  customer is, via their mobile device
- **geotargeting** — the specific method of doing that by matching the device's
  IP address to a location
- **social media advertising (SMA)** — online advertising through social
  platforms to deliver targeted commercial messages
- **reach** — the number of people exposed to an advertisement's message
- **frequency** — the average number of times someone is exposed to it

## Distinctions and confusions

- **Reach is not frequency.** Reach counts people, frequency counts exposures per
  person. A campaign can have high reach and frequency of one, which is a
  different strategy from low reach at frequency ten. Students use the two words
  as synonyms and lose the distinction that makes them measurable.
- **E-marketing is not promotion moved online.** It changes the process and the
  physical evidence as well. A paragraph that only discusses advertising has
  covered a third of the element.
- **Having a website is not e-marketing.** The source's own criticism of
  Australian retailers is precise: many offer product information with no
  ability to buy. Presence without transaction is not the strategy.
- **Cheap is not free.** The advantage claimed for SMA is cost relative to
  traditional advertising, not absence of cost. It still needs daily attention,
  which is a labour cost a small business feels.
- **Location-based marketing is not the same as targeting by demographics.** It
  targets by current position, which only pays off where proximity changes the
  purchase decision.

## Instructional implications (USE)

1. name the target market characteristic that makes an online channel the right
   one
2. name the specific technology, not "social media" in general
3. state the mechanism: what the technology does that reaches this market
4. give the case detail and say what it demonstrates
5. name the cost of the choice, most usefully the loss of control or the
   attention it demands

Step 5 is where an "explain" answer starts to sound like an "evaluate" one, and
it is available here because the source states the disadvantage plainly.

## Source gaps

- No causal link from target market to channel choice. The technologies are
  catalogued, not chosen.
- The figures given (online spend, proportion of shoppers, platform user counts,
  advertising revenues) are dated and unsourced in a form we could cite. They are
  useful as background for authoring and unusable as student evidence until
  someone verifies the primary source.
- No treatment of cost, measurement or return, beyond the assertion that SMA is
  inexpensive and measurable. Nothing a student could use to argue *how much*
  better one channel is than another.
- The case studies illustrate enthusiasm for a channel rather than a decision
  between channels, so none of them models a trade-off.
