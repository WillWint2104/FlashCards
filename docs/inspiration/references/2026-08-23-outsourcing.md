## Concepts (SOURCE)

**Outsourcing** is using external providers to perform business activities. The
theory behind it: a provider that specialises in one function performs it more
cheaply and more effectively than the same work done inside the business
hierarchy.

The general term is **business process outsourcing (BPO)**, which covers
operations (manufacturing, design, merchandising, sourcing, distribution,
logistics), human resources (payroll, training, counselling, expenses),
administration and back-office work, and information technology. Named
subtypes: **FAO** (finance and accounting), **KPO** (knowledge process, meaning
managerial work such as marketing strategy and public relations), **LPO** (legal
process).

Two axes classify any arrangement:

- **where**: onshore (same country), near-shore (a nearby country in the
  region), offshore (further afield)
- **who owns it**: captive or in-house (the business's own facility elsewhere)
  against non-captive (a third-party vendor)

The two axes are independent, which is the point of the classification: a
business can have an offshore captive centre or an onshore third-party vendor.

## Explanation (SOURCE)

**The decision.** Four things have to be settled, in order:

1. whether outsourcing is cheaper and more efficient than doing the work in
   house at all
2. if so, which geographical location
3. which vendor
4. the contract itself: its length, the KPIs, the service levels, and how the
   relationship will be managed

Point 4 is the one students skip and the one that determines whether it works.

**Four options**, running from lowest to highest commitment:

| option | what it is |
| --- | --- |
| shared services centre | an in-house centre performing the work for multiple subsidiaries; outsourcing without leaving the business |
| fee for service | engaging a supplier for fixed services at a set price; low risk, short term, a way to test the market before committing |
| joint venture | using a provider that also serves competitors in the same industry, so the business gains the expertise that provider built elsewhere |
| build operate transfer | building an offshore operation, running it, then transferring it to an independent vendor under contract |

**Advantages.**

- *Simplification*, from performing fewer activities internally.
- *Cost savings*, from cheaper labour, regulatory differences and available
  skilled labour offshore.
- *Increased process capability*, from access to better technology and skills
  than the business has, which shows up as better service levels.
- *Increased accountability*, through **service level agreements** that
  contractually bind the vendor to targets on KPIs. This one is
  counter-intuitive and worth teaching: outsourcing can make performance *more*
  measurable, because it has to be written down.
- *Access to skills the business lacks*, with a double saving where the skills
  already exist in the vendor's labour market, since the business avoids the
  training cost as well.
- *Focus on core competencies*, meaning what cannot be outsourced: the vision,
  the source of competitive advantage, the innovation.
- *Strategic benefits*, four of them: getting around trade barriers by becoming
  a local supplier; gaining expertise a vendor built serving competitors;
  exploiting time zones so processing happens overnight; and a strong vendor
  partnership generating innovations the business would not have found.
- *Improved in-house performance*, because attention that was spread across
  everything can now be spent on what remains.

**Disadvantages.**

- *Payback takes years.* Savings are real but may take two or three years to
  repay the cost of reorganising. And the alternative is live: a business that
  removed its own internal inefficiencies might reach the same productivity
  without outsourcing at all.
- *Communication, language and culture.* The relationship typically lasts three
  to five years, and businesses over-focus on the decision and under-plan the
  relationship. Differences in language, culture and how problems are handled
  cause misalignment, including disagreement about what the agreed service
  levels actually mean.
- *Loss of control over standards and information security.* A vendor that does
  not follow the specification produces a defect the brand owns, not the vendor.
  Privacy and data handling may be weaker outside the business than inside it.
- *New hierarchies.* Outsourcing aimed at removing the cost of hierarchy can
  create its own, because complex agreements need managing.
- *Organisational change and job loss.* Downsizing, domestic redundancy, and
  reputational damage. Job migration to the vendor is theoretically possible and
  often blocked by work visas.
- *Loss of corporate memory.* Knowledge of how processes work leaves with the
  process. Some businesses counter this with **shadow teams** that retain the
  knowledge and the capability to bring the work back. Vendors may also be
  required to hold contingency plans against a regional crisis.
- *IT cost.* Adapting systems to the vendor takes money and time, which can
  cancel the short-term financial advantage.

## Relationships and mechanisms (DERIVED unless marked)

Outsourcing is a **cause** acting on performance objectives, and it is the
strategy where the trade-off is most visible because it is fundamentally a
transfer: the business gives up control to gain cost or capability.

| what outsourcing buys | what it pays |
| --- | --- |
| cost, through cheaper labour and regulatory difference | quality of conformance, since the specification is now enforced by contract rather than by supervision |
| process capability and quality, where the vendor is better at it | dependability, since a disruption in the vendor's country is a disruption to the business |
| flexibility, since capacity can be scaled by contract rather than by hiring | flexibility, since a three to five year contract is a commitment the business cannot quickly reverse |
| speed, where time zones let work continue overnight | speed of response, since a change has to be negotiated rather than instructed |

That flexibility row is the sharpest thing in the topic and the source never
states it: outsourcing is *sold* as flexibility and *contracted* as rigidity.
Which one it turns out to be depends entirely on the contract length against how
fast the market moves.

The concentration risk is the other point worth authoring. A business with
operations in three countries believes it is diversified until a shock hits all
three, at which point it has to rebuild domestic capability it deliberately
dismantled. The source's own case shows exactly this: a business that had to
retrain and recruit two thousand domestic staff when its offshore centres could
not operate. That is the evidence a student needs for the concentration argument,
and it is a case study rather than an explanation.

Outsourcing also links straight into ops-03. The reputational objection to
outsourcing is a corporate social responsibility argument, and the source makes
the uncomfortable observation that the consumers who criticise it are frequently
the ones buying the cheaper product it produces. That tension is exactly what a
"to what extent" question is looking for.

## Terminology (SOURCE)

- **outsourcing** — using external providers to perform business activities
- **business process outsourcing (BPO)** — the general term for outsourcing a
  business process
- **FAO / KPO / LPO** — finance and accounting, knowledge process, legal process
  outsourcing
- **onshore / near-shore / offshore** — the same country, a nearby country in
  the region, a more distant one
- **captive** — an outsourcing arrangement the business still owns
- **non-captive** — outsourced to a third-party vendor through the market
- **vendor** — the external provider
- **service level agreement (SLA)** — a contract binding the vendor to
  pre-agreed KPIs and service levels
- **shared services centre (SSC)** — an in-house centre serving multiple
  subsidiaries
- **build operate transfer** — building an offshore operation and later
  transferring it to an independent vendor
- **core competency** — what the business does that gives it advantage, and so
  what it will not outsource
- **shadow team** — an internal team kept to retain knowledge of an outsourced
  process
- **corporate memory** — accumulated knowledge of how the business's processes
  work

## Distinctions and confusions

- **Outsourcing is not offshoring.** Outsourcing is about who performs the work;
  offshoring is about where it is performed. An onshore vendor is outsourcing
  without offshoring; a captive offshore centre is offshoring without
  outsourcing.
- **Outsourcing is not global sourcing.** Global sourcing buys inputs. Outsourcing
  hands over an activity.
- **Losing control is not losing responsibility.** A defect produced by a vendor
  is still the brand's defect in the customer's eyes and in law. This is the
  single most useful point for an evaluate question.
- **Core competencies are what is kept, not what is sent.** Students write that
  a business outsources its core activities to specialists. The logic runs the
  other way: the core is what cannot be handed over without losing the
  advantage.
- **Cost saving is not immediate.** Two to three years to payback, and IT
  adaptation can consume the early gain. An answer claiming instant savings has
  missed the main disadvantage the source gives.
- **SLAs do not guarantee compliance.** They make failure contractually
  actionable after the fact. The lead-in-toys example is a vendor breaching a
  specification that was written down.

## Instructional implications (USE)

1. name what is outsourced and to whom, using the axes: onshore or offshore,
   captive or third party
2. name the performance objective the business was pursuing
3. state the mechanism, in terms of what the vendor can do that the business
   cannot, or cannot do as cheaply
4. name the control the business gave up, and the risk that comes with it
5. judge whether the trade held, ideally against a moment when it was tested

Step 5 is available on this topic in a way it is not on others, because
outsourcing arrangements get stress-tested by real disruptions and the results
are documented.
