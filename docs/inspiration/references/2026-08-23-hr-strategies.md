## Concepts (SOURCE)

Eight strategies, each a syllabus dot point with its own sub-choice:

| strategy | the choice inside it |
| --- | --- |
| **leadership style** | autocratic through to participative, on a continuum |
| **job design** | general or specific tasks |
| **recruitment** | internal or external; general or specific skills |
| **training and development** | current or future skills |
| **performance management** | developmental or administrative |
| **rewards** | monetary or non-monetary; individual or group; performance pay |
| **global strategies** | costs, skills, supply |
| **workplace disputes** | negotiation, mediation, grievance procedures, courts and tribunals |

Every one is a *choice between two positions*, not a thing a business either does
or does not do. That structure is the topic.

## Explanation (SOURCE)

**Leadership style** is how managers communicate with employees to inspire and
motivate them toward the organisation's goals. It runs on a continuum: management
decides and tells; decides and invites questions; presents the problem, asks for
suggestions and decides; presents the problem and decides jointly with staff.

*Autocratic (authoritarian)* means quick decisions, often without staff input. It
works with unskilled or inexperienced workers where work is highly organised and
controlled, in a crisis, where fast decisions are needed, and where the nature of
the work requires strong centralised control. The chapter names the fast food
industry as a current example. Its inflexibility can raise absenteeism and staff
turnover. It aligns with a *transactional* management style, where compliance is
recognised through financial reward tied to organisational goals, which suits
people driven by financial reward and is often linked to lower job satisfaction.
Its origins are in the Classical school of management.

*Participative (democratic)* is consultative, engaging workers in decision
making. It aligns with *transformational* leadership, where higher expectations
of workers produce higher engagement. Involvement gives workers ownership of the
decision and produces higher job satisfaction and productivity. It works where
the emphasis is on quality of output rather than efficiency. Its costs: decision
making takes longer, and where workers are inexperienced or disaffected their
input may add little.

The chapter's conclusion is that most managers use several styles and that
effective leaders are flexible, analysing the situation and choosing accordingly.

**Job design** is the number, kind and variety of tasks a worker performs.
*Specific tasks* are routine, repetitive and inflexible, represented by the
Scientific management approach: one best way of doing a job, worker skills matched
to job requirements, built on specialisation, efficient, often using low-skilled
cheap labour. *General tasks* widen the variety, sometimes called **job
enlargement**, the horizontal expansion of a job by adding responsibilities at
the same level, aimed at engagement, satisfaction and productivity. The warning
attached is that managers must not add tasks for their own sake, and that
specialisation has real benefits.

The elements of a well designed job: variety of tasks, discretion and autonomy,
challenge, task identity, social interaction, flexibility, resources, and
opportunity for ongoing learning. Employees are more motivated where they have
autonomy, clear task identity, competence through training, and feedback. The
distinction the chapter draws about feedback is precise and useful: feedback is
demotivating when it is *controlling* and motivating when it is *informative and
constructive*.

**Recruitment** is locating and attracting the right quantity and quality of
staff for vacancies at the right cost. What determines the method: the business's
recruitment goals and policies, labour market conditions, location, financial
resources, and whether the job needs general or specific skills.

*Internal* — advantages: motivational, builds commitment and loyalty, the
business only hires at base level, employees already know the culture so
productivity is maintained, supports succession planning, recognises effort,
cheaper, and less risk of failure because strengths and weaknesses are already
observed. Disadvantages: can reinforce a negative culture, creates rivalry,
requires an established training and merit-based appraisal framework, produces
unsuccessful internal applicants who are demotivated, and adds no new skills.

*External* — advantages: wider applicant pool, new ideas and perspectives,
specific skills without training cost, dilutes internal politics, more diversity,
builds the organisational brand, and the new employee can be shaped. Disadvantages:
unknown staff, lost productivity during induction, possible culture misfit and
rejection by internal rivals, time and effort, and risk of legal claims.

The cost of getting it wrong is quantified: a poor recruitment decision is
generally believed to cost around two to three times the annual salary of the
person recruited. A poor process raises training costs, dissatisfaction, turnover,
absenteeism, accident and defect rates, and discrimination claims.

**Training and development.** *Training* develops skills, knowledge and attitudes
leading to superior work performance, and the chapter frames it against a stated
labour market problem: a shortage of skilled labour and a mismatch between skills
needed and skills available. *Development* enhances skills in line with the
changing and future needs of the business, and encourages employees to build a
career there. The business benefits by retaining experience and knowledge and by
maintaining competitiveness.

**Performance management** addresses individual and business performance
together, on the premise that successful individual performance translates into
strategic objectives being met. Job design, recruitment, training and rewards are
all part of it. Two purposes:

- *developmental* — using data to develop individual skills and abilities,
  overcome weaknesses and prepare people for promotion, best achieved through
  year-round periodic feedback and shared, empathetic, goal-focused discussion
- *administrative* — using the same data for decisions about pay, hours,
  promotion and continued employment

The chapter notes that the most admired businesses treat human resource
performance measures as equal to financial ones and review them at board level.

**Rewards.** *Monetary* rewards have financial value; *non-monetary* do not.
Direct monetary: base pay, incentive pay such as bonuses and commissions,
allowances such as overtime and shiftwork, pay increases, share plans, profit
sharing, gainsharing. Indirect monetary (benefits): insurance, superannuation,
medical and health, childcare, employee assistance, flexible work schedules,
holidays. Non-monetary job rewards: interesting work, challenge, responsibility,
recognition. **Remuneration** covers both the financial and non-financial benefits
received in return for work effort. Reward policy reinforces culture and values
and is linked to sustainable competitive advantage.

**Global strategies** turn on costs, skills and supply. Higher Australian living
standards bring expectations of higher wages, plus on-costs such as
superannuation. Some businesses restructure, outsourcing functions to lower labour
cost countries or establishing offshore **subsidiaries** to cut production costs
and reach new markets. The chapter gives Australian instances of manufacturing
and maintenance work relocating offshore with stated job losses, and of
professional processing work moving to countries with large, English-speaking,
tertiary-educated labour pools at lower rates. On skills, managers report a
mismatch between the skills workers have and those business needs, an ongoing
skills shortage, and disagreement about whether the cause is the education system
or employers preferring to import cheaper labour or recruit overseas.

**Workplace disputes.** Disputes are conflicts, disagreements or dissatisfaction
between individuals or groups, and conflict between stakeholders is described as
inevitable, particularly over how far employees share in profits. Unresolved
conflict raises absenteeism, lowers productivity, and produces legal claims and
turnover.

An **industrial dispute** is a disagreement between employer and employees that
results in employees ceasing work. **Strikes** are workers withdrawing labour, the
most overt form, aimed at publicity and support. **Lockouts** are employers closing
the workplace and refusing admission. **Pickets** are protests outside the
workplace. Legal claims for discrimination, harassment and bullying are not
counted as industrial disputes.

Three resolution processes, in order of who decides:

1. **negotiated** — the parties work out the solution themselves
2. **mediated** — an independent mediator assists them to reach agreement
3. **arbitrated or adjudicated** — an independent arbitrator or court determines
   the matter and makes a legally binding order

Stakeholders involved: employees, employers, governments, trade unions, employer
associations, courts and industrial tribunals. Parties are expected to meet **in
good faith**, meaning with a willingness to reach agreement.

## Load-bearing derived claims (DERIVED)

```
SOURCE  Autocratic leadership works with unskilled or inexperienced workers where
        work is highly organised and controlled, and the chapter names fast food as
        a current example. It also states that autocratic style is linked to higher
        absenteeism and staff turnover.
DERIVED Those two facts describe one trade, not two observations. A business that
        chooses specific job design and centrally set procedures has already chosen
        the conditions in which autocratic leadership works, and has accepted the
        turnover that comes with it. Leadership style is downstream of job design,
        not independent of it.
USE     The strongest structural insight available for hr-01, because it explains why
        a business cannot simply adopt a more participative style to fix turnover
        while keeping the job design that produced it.
```

```
SOURCE  A poor recruitment decision is stated to cost around two to three times the
        recruited person's annual salary. Internal recruitment is listed as cheaper
        with less chance of failure because strengths and weaknesses are known.
DERIVED That figure is what makes the internal-versus-external choice a financial
        decision rather than a preference. External recruitment buys new skills and
        pays for them twice: once in the search and again in the risk of a mismatch
        priced at multiples of salary.
USE     Converts a two-column advantages table into a judgement with a magnitude
        attached, which is what "evaluate" requires.
```

```
SOURCE  Performance management can be developmental or administrative, and the same
        data serves both.
DERIVED Using one data set for both purposes undermines the developmental one. An
        employee who knows an appraisal decides their hours has an incentive to
        conceal weakness, which is exactly what developmental feedback needs
        surfaced. The chapter never notes that the two purposes conflict.
USE     A genuine limitation for any performance management pathway, and one a
        student can apply to any case rather than memorise.
```

```
SOURCE  Non-monetary rewards are defined as those without financial value, and
        include flexible work schedules, interesting work, challenge, responsibility
        and recognition.
DERIVED Non-monetary rewards are cheap for the business and are the only rewards a
        business bound by an award wage floor can actually vary. Where pay is set
        externally, the entire reward strategy has to run through the non-monetary
        column.
USE     Explains why two businesses paying identical award rates can have very
        different retention, which is the mechanism hr-01 wants.
```

```
SOURCE  Three dispute resolution processes are listed in order: negotiated,
        mediated, arbitrated.
DERIVED The order is a loss of control. Each step hands more of the decision to
        someone outside the business, so a business that resolves disputes early
        keeps authorship of the outcome, and one that escalates gives it away.
USE     Turns a list of three procedures into a reason to prefer the first, which is
        what an "evaluate the effectiveness" answer needs.
```

## Relationships and mechanisms (DERIVED unless marked)

The eight strategies are the **cause** end of hr-01, and business performance is
the effect end. But the strategies are not independent, and that is the
distinctive feature of this topic compared with operations or finance.

**The strategies form a consistent bundle.** Two coherent configurations exist,
and most real businesses sit near one of them:

| the specialisation configuration | the engagement configuration |
| --- | --- |
| specific job design, narrow tasks | general job design, enlarged roles |
| autocratic or directive leadership | participative leadership |
| external recruitment at base level for general skills | internal recruitment and promotion |
| training in current skills, short and standardised | development for future skills, longer |
| administrative performance management | developmental performance management |
| monetary rewards at the award floor | non-monetary rewards and career reward |
| high turnover accepted and planned for | turnover minimised as a cost |

Neither is wrong. The first suits high-volume standardised work where a new
employee must be productive quickly; the second suits work where judgement
carries the value. The mistake is mixing them: participative leadership over
specific job design gives workers a voice about decisions that were already made
centrally, and developmental appraisal in a business with award-floor pay creates
expectations it cannot reward.

**Against business performance**, the mechanisms differ by strategy:

| strategy | how it reaches performance |
| --- | --- |
| leadership | speed and consistency of decisions, against engagement and turnover |
| job design | time to productivity and quality consistency, against motivation |
| recruitment | fit and skill supply, against cost and risk of mismatch |
| training | capability and quality, against cost and the risk of training someone who leaves |
| performance management | alignment of individual to business objectives, against the honesty of the feedback |
| rewards | attraction, motivation and retention, against wage cost |
| global strategies | labour cost and access to skills, against control, distance and reputational exposure |
| dispute resolution | continuity of operation, against the precedent each settlement sets |

**The training paradox** is worth authoring explicitly because it is where HR meets
finance. Training raises capability and raises the employee's value in the
external labour market, so the better the training the greater the risk the
employee leaves. The business's defence is development and a career path, which is
why training and development appear as one dot point rather than two.

**Global strategies are the point where HR becomes an ethical question.** The
chapter gives job-loss figures for relocations without discussing them, and the
same decision that lowers labour cost removes domestic employment. That connects
straight to corporate social responsibility and gives hr-01 its evaluate step.

## Trade-offs and conditions (DERIVED)

- **Specialisation against motivation.** Fast to productivity, consistent
  quality, and repetition causes boredom. Rotation is the partial fix and it
  costs training time.
- **Internal against external recruitment.** Loyalty and cheapness against new
  skills and diversity. The source states both columns and never says which
  condition tips it: the answer is whether the business needs capability it does
  not have.
- **Autocratic against participative.** Speed against engagement. Conditional on
  workforce experience and on whether the work rewards judgement.
- **Monetary against non-monetary reward.** Money is universal and expensive;
  non-monetary is cheap and works only where it matches what that employee wants.
- **Developmental against administrative appraisal.** Honesty against
  accountability, and one system cannot fully do both.
- **Global labour cost against control and reputation.** The saving is immediate
  and the reputational cost arrives later and unevenly.

## Terminology (SOURCE)

- **leadership style** — how managers communicate to inspire and motivate toward
  organisational goals
- **autocratic / authoritarian** — decisions made quickly, often without input
- **participative / democratic** — consultative, engaging workers in decisions
- **transactional** — compliance recognised through financial reward
- **transformational** — higher expectations producing higher engagement
- **job design** — the number, kind and variety of tasks a worker performs
- **job enlargement** — horizontal expansion by adding same-level responsibilities
- **specialisation** — narrowing a job to a small set of repeated tasks
- **recruitment** — locating and attracting the right quantity and quality of
  staff at the right cost
- **internal / external recruitment** — filling vacancies from inside or outside
- **training** — developing skills, knowledge and attitudes for superior
  performance
- **development** — enhancing skills for the changing and future needs of the
  business
- **performance management** — addressing individual and business performance
  together
- **developmental / administrative** — appraisal used to build capability, or to
  decide pay, hours and continued employment
- **monetary / non-monetary rewards** — rewards with, or without, financial value
- **remuneration** — the financial and non-financial benefits received for work
- **gainsharing** — sharing gains from productivity improvement with employees
- **subsidiary** — a company owned by a parent, often in another country
- **industrial dispute** — a disagreement resulting in employees ceasing work
- **strike / lockout / picket** — labour withdrawn; workplace closed to workers;
  protest outside the workplace
- **negotiation / mediation / arbitration** — the parties decide; a mediator
  assists; an independent party decides and binds
- **in good faith** — meeting with a willingness to reach agreement

## Distinctions and confusions

- **SOURCE. Training is not development.** Training is current skills for the
  current role; development is future skills for the changing needs of the
  business and the employee's career.
- **SOURCE. Feedback is not automatically motivating.** Controlling feedback
  demotivates; informative and constructive feedback motivates.
- **DERIVED. Leadership style is not personality.** It is a strategy chosen for
  conditions, and the same manager may use several.
- **DERIVED. Job enlargement is not job enrichment.** Enlargement adds tasks at
  the same level, horizontally. Enrichment adds responsibility and autonomy,
  vertically. The chapter defines enlargement and students supply enrichment.
- **DERIVED. Non-monetary is not worthless.** It has no financial value to the
  employee as cash and real value as a reward, which is precisely why it is the
  lever available under a fixed wage.
- **DERIVED. An industrial dispute is not any workplace conflict.** It requires
  employees to cease work. Discrimination, harassment and bullying claims are
  excluded from the statistics.
- **DERIVED. Arbitration is not the goal of dispute resolution.** It is the
  outcome when the first two fail, and it means the business no longer decides.
- **DERIVED. A strategy is not effective in itself.** hr-01 asks for
  effectiveness in improving business performance, so a strategy that improved
  satisfaction without reaching performance has not answered the question.

## Instructional implications (USE)

Paragraph shape for hr-01, an evaluate question:

1. name the strategy and which position on its internal choice the business took
2. name the business performance measure it was aimed at
3. state the mechanism: what changes about how work is done or who does it
4. give the indicator that would show it worked, and the result if there is one
5. name what the choice cost, using the trade-off for that strategy
6. judge, and say whether the strategy fitted the rest of the bundle

Step 6 is what this topic offers that the others do not. A strategy can be well
executed and still wrong because it contradicts the job design or the reward
structure around it, and saying so is a level of argument most students never
reach.

Candidate pathways: job design to time-to-productivity, with boredom as the cost;
internal recruitment to retention, with the no-new-skills cost; training to
quality, with the leaver risk; non-monetary rewards to retention under a fixed
wage floor; leadership style to consistency, with turnover as the cost; dispute
resolution level to continuity, with loss of control as the cost.

  constrain each other, so the idea that they form a consistent bundle is entirely
  ours.
- Leadership style and job design are treated as independent choices when the
