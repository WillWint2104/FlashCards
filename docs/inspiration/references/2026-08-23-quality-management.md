## Concepts (SOURCE)

**Quality** is the degree of excellence of a product and its fitness for a
stated purpose. **Quality management** is the set of processes a business uses
to make its output consistent, reliable, safe and fit for purpose.

Three approaches, and the difference between them is *when* they act:

- **quality control (QC)** — inspection at points in the process, after
  something has been made. Reactive.
- **quality assurance (QA)** — a system built to prevent defects, usually
  certified against an external standard. Proactive.
- **quality improvement (QI)** — continuous improvement and total quality
  management, aimed at the process itself rather than the output. Ongoing.

## Explanation (SOURCE)

**Quality control.** The business defines standards and applies them across its
products and processes, designs tests against those standards, sets targets, and
investigates any failure to meet them. Inspection can cover all output or a
sample. The simplest form is an **attribute inspection**: the item passes or it
is defective. Does the switch turn on and off? Does the container leak?

QC applies to services too, and this is the version students underuse. A bank
inspecting teller accuracy, courtesy and speed, or a supermarket checking
scanning rates, is running quality control on a service. The customer service
example the source gives makes the point sharply: excellent stock sold by an
inattentive salesperson still loses the sale, so the quality being controlled is
the interaction, not the goods.

QC requires trained labour, because a standard nobody has been taught to apply
is not being applied.

**Quality assurance.** QA moves the effort upstream into design and system, so
defects do not occur rather than being caught. Two ideas carry it: **fitness for
purpose** (how well the product does what it was designed to do) and **right
first time** (avoiding rework, which wastes time, energy and resources).

QA is usually expressed through certification against an international standard,
most commonly the ISO 9001 series. The reason those standards exist is
globalisation: when components for one product are made in several countries, a
universally applied standard means a component meeting it in one country is
theoretically equivalent to one meeting it in another. Certification is
voluntary. Businesses take it because it makes them credible to domestic and
international buyers, which makes it as much a marketing asset as an operations
one.

**Quality improvement.** Continuous improvement is the commitment that processes
will keep getting better, whether through one large innovation or by accumulation
of small changes. Its defining feature is that all staff are expected to
contribute, so ownership of quality is distributed rather than held by an
inspection function.

**Six Sigma** is the structured version. It is a measurement-based method aimed
at near perfection, defined as 3.4 defective parts per million, or 99.9997 per
cent. It uses statistical tools to find variation in a process, trained staff to
run projects, and the **DMAIC** cycle: define the goals that satisfy customer
demand, measure the characteristics critical to quality, analyse the causes of
defects, improve the process to reduce them, control the process so the gains
hold. Trained project leaders are the "black belts" and "green belts", with
managers acting as project champions.

The mechanism Six Sigma actually works by is worth stating precisely, because
students describe the belts and miss it: **it looks for sources of variation and
either eliminates them or brings them under control.** Variation is the target,
not defect count. Defects are the symptom.

## Relationships and mechanisms (DERIVED unless marked)

Quality management is a **cause** acting on performance objectives, and unlike
inventory it acts on several of them in the same direction, which makes it the
easier strategy to argue and the harder one to argue *well*.

- It raises **quality of conformance** directly, by definition.
- It raises **dependability**, because reducing variation is exactly what makes
  output consistent every time.
- It lowers **cost** over time, because right-first-time removes rework, scrap
  and warranty claims. The source's own case reports a construction materials
  division identifying tens of millions in cost reduction initiatives.
- It raises **speed** indirectly, because unplanned stoppages and rework are
  time.

The trade-off, which the source never states, is in the direction students
overlook: **quality management costs before it saves.** Inspection is labour.
Certification is an audit fee and a compliance burden. Six Sigma is training,
statistical capability and a team of people not making anything. A business
chooses it when the cost of a defect reaching a customer exceeds the cost of
preventing it, and that condition does not hold everywhere. This is why the
approach differs by business rather than every business running Six Sigma.

The three approaches also trade off against each other:

- QC is cheap to start and expensive to sustain, because it never reduces the
  defect rate, it only catches defects.
- QA is expensive to establish and cheap to run, and it is the one that
  transfers credibility to buyers who cannot inspect for themselves.
- QI is the only one that changes the process, and the only one that requires a
  culture rather than a procedure, which is why it fails most often.

The link to human resources is load-bearing here. Continuous improvement depends
on staff raising issues, and jidoka gives any worker authority to halt
production. Both are HR conditions, not operations ones. A quality strategy in a
business whose staff are afraid to report problems does not work.

## Terminology (SOURCE)

- **quality** — degree of excellence and fitness for a stated purpose
- **quality management** — processes ensuring consistency, reliability, safety
  and fitness for purpose
- **quality control (QC)** — inspection at points in the process to find
  problems and defects
- **attribute inspection** — a pass or defective judgement on an item
- **quality assurance (QA)** — a system ensuring set standards are achieved in
  production
- **fitness for purpose** — how well a product does what it was designed to do
- **right first time** — producing without the need for rework
- **ISO 9001** — the widely used international series of quality certifications;
  voluntary, and adopted for competitiveness
- **quality improvement (QI)** — continuous improvement and total quality
  management
- **continuous improvement** — an ongoing commitment to improving goods or
  services, with all staff contributing
- **total quality management** — quality as a whole-of-business responsibility
  rather than an inspection function
- **Six Sigma** — a measurement-based method targeting 3.4 defects per million
- **DMAIC** — define, measure, analyse, improve, control
- **variation** — inconsistency in a process; the actual target of statistical
  quality methods

## Distinctions and confusions

- **Quality control is not quality assurance.** QC inspects output after the
  fact; QA designs the system so the fault does not occur. Reactive against
  proactive is the distinction, and it is the one most often asked for.
- **Quality is not expense.** A product can be high quality at a low
  specification if it consistently meets that specification. Fitness for purpose
  is measured against the stated purpose, not against the best product available.
- **Certification is not quality.** ISO 9001 certifies that a system exists and
  is followed. It says nothing about whether the standard the system enforces is
  a good one.
- **Continuous improvement is not incremental only.** The source allows for a
  single large innovation as well as gradual change. Students define it as small
  steps and then cannot classify a breakthrough.
- **Six Sigma is not a synonym for quality management.** It is one QI method,
  measurement-heavy and expensive to establish, suited to high-volume processes
  where variation can be measured statistically. It does not fit every business.
- **Quality management applies to services.** Every worked example students meet
  is a factory, so they arrive at a service question with nothing to say.

## Instructional implications (USE)

1. name the quality approach and which of the three families it belongs to
2. name the performance objective it serves, in the objective's own vocabulary
3. state the mechanism, and prefer variation to defects: what inconsistency was
   removed, and how
4. give the outcome as a measured change, not an assertion of improvement
5. name what it cost to establish, and why this business could justify it

Step 3 is what separates an answer that has understood quality management from
one that has memorised three headings. Step 5 is the one the source never
models.
