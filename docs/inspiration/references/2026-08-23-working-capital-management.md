## Concepts (SOURCE)

**Working capital** is the funds available for a business's short-term financial
commitments. **Net working capital** is current assets minus current
liabilities: the funds needed for day-to-day operation, to produce profit and to
provide cash for short-term liquidity.

**Working capital management** is determining the best mix of current assets and
current liabilities to achieve the business's objectives. The balance being
struck is stated plainly by the source and is the whole topic: using funds to
create profit against holding enough funds to cover payments.

The syllabus splits it three ways:

- **control of current assets** — cash, receivables, inventories
- **control of current liabilities** — payables, loans, overdrafts
- **strategies** — leasing, sale and lease-back

## Explanation (SOURCE)

**Why short-term liquidity matters.** Enough of it lets a business take profit
opportunities when they appear, meet obligations, pay creditors early enough to
claim discounts, pay tax, and meet loan and overdraft payments. Too little
forces the sale of non-current assets such as property and equipment to raise
cash, which reduces profitability for owners and shareholders in the long run.
Creditors watch liquidity closely because it tells them whether they will be
paid; failing to pay on time alienates suppliers, who then carry collection costs
and lose confidence in the business.

**The working capital cycle.** The time it takes to convert current assets and
liabilities back into cash: from buying inventory or raw materials, through the
sale, to receiving the cash. The length of that cycle is what determines how much
working capital a business needs, and it varies enormously by business. A food
outlet buys stock and sells it within days, so its cycle is short and it needs
little working capital. A manufacturer that buys raw materials, holds work in
progress, finishes goods, sells on credit and then waits for payment has a long
cycle and needs far more.

That is the answer to "how much working capital should a business hold", and it
is a better answer than any ratio: **the cycle length sets the requirement.**

**Both directions fail.** Insufficient working capital means cash shortages and
liquidity problems, forcing the business to take on debt, find new finance, or
sell non-current assets. Excess working capital means assets are earning less
than it costs to finance them. Business failure can come from either.

**The current (working capital) ratio** is current assets divided by current
liabilities, and it shows whether current assets cover current liabilities. It
indicates the risk the business is carrying in the trade between profitability
and liquidity.

**Control of current assets.** Current assets may be around 40 per cent of a
business's assets, and they change constantly as inventory sells, cash goes out
and payments come in. Control means choosing the optimal level of each and
financing it. Excess inventory and uncontrolled receivables create unused assets,
raising cost and creating liquidity problems; idle cash costs; and too *little*
inventory or too tight a credit policy causes its own problems.

- **Cash.** Enough to pay debts, repay loans, settle accounts, and survive.
  Reserves also let management take investment opportunities. Businesses usually
  keep cash balances to a minimum and hold **marketable securities** as the
  liquidity reserve instead, so the buffer earns something. Planning the timing
  of receipts, payments and asset purchases with a cash flow forecast is what
  avoids both shortage and excess.
- **Receivables.** Money owed by customers for goods already delivered, so the
  sale is made and only the cash is outstanding. The faster debtors pay, the
  better the cash position. The management procedures: set the right credit terms
  (typical cycles run 30 to 90 days, longer extends more credit, shorter gets
  paid faster), offer varied payment methods, reward early payment with discounts
  or other incentives, charge late payment fees, credit-check prospective
  customers, issue statements monthly on the same day, follow up overdue
  accounts, and have a policy for bad debts including debt collection. An **aged
  debtors report** groups what is owed by how overdue it is, which identifies
  slow payers before they are given more credit.
- **Inventories.** Monitored so neither excess nor insufficient stock occurs.
  This is the point where working capital management and operations are the same
  decision.

**Control of current liabilities.** Minimising the cost of what must be repaid
short term.

- **Payables.** Holding accounts payable until their final due date is a cheap
  way to improve liquidity, because suppliers commonly allow an interest-free
  **trade credit** period. Thirty days to pay is thirty days of cost-free
  finance. Against that, discounts for early settlement reduce cost. So the
  business chooses between two savings, and the right answer depends on its cash
  position. Accounts must still be paid by the due date, or late charges apply and
  future trade credit may be withdrawn. Control means periodically reviewing
  suppliers and what they offer: discounts, interest-free periods, extended
  terms. Alternative arrangements exist for slow-turnover stock: **floor plan
  finance**, where the supplier provides goods before payment is due, and
  **consignment finance**, where payment is generally not required until the
  goods sell and unsold goods can be returned.
- **Loans.** Short-term borrowing for property transactions, unforeseen events,
  and import or export commitments. Short-term loans are generally expensive and
  their use should be minimised. Control means investigating establishment costs,
  interest rates and ongoing charges across institutions, and keeping a
  relationship with lenders so the right facility is available.
- **Overdrafts.** Convenient and relatively cheap short-term borrowing to cover
  temporary shortages. Interest is usually lower than on a loan. The bank can
  demand immediate repayment, though rarely where the record and relationship are
  good. Fees vary by overdraft type, so a business needs a policy for using them
  and should monitor budgets daily or weekly.

**Strategies.**

*Leasing* is paying for the use of an asset owned by someone else: a contract
between the **lessor**, who owns it, and the **lessee**, who uses it, for
periodic payments over a term. Its advantages, and they are mostly working
capital advantages:

- payments spread over years instead of one large outflow to buy the asset
- access to good quality assets the business could not afford to buy
- lease payments are operating expenses and so tax deductible
- flexibility to upgrade when the asset is outdated, without a large outlay
- reduced risk of technological obsolescence, since the asset can be upgraded
- depending on terms, reduced exposure to unpredictable repair and maintenance
  costs
- fixed payments for a known term, which makes forecasting and budgeting easier
  than borrowing whose repayments fluctuate

*Sale and lease-back* is selling an asset the business already owns to a lessor
and then leasing it back under fixed payments for a period. The business keeps
using the asset and converts it into cash.

## Relationships and mechanisms (DERIVED unless marked)

Working capital management is a **cause** acting on the financial objectives, and
it is the strategy that touches the most of them at once.

| decision | objective raised | objective paid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| holding more cash and inventory | liquidity | profitability and efficiency, since idle assets earn less than they cost |
| tightening credit terms | liquidity, efficiency | profitability, because customers may buy elsewhere |
| delaying payables to the due date | liquidity, at zero cost | nothing, until it becomes lateness, which costs charges and future credit |
| leasing rather than buying | liquidity, and flexibility against obsolescence | profitability over the asset's life, since leasing generally costs more in total |
| sale and lease-back | liquidity, immediately | ownership of the asset, and profitability over the lease term |

Two connections are worth authoring explicitly because they are where fin-01
answers get their strongest content:

**Leasing and sale and lease-back raise liquidity without raising gearing.** The
business gets the use of an asset, or cash for one it owns, without borrowing.
For a business already highly geared, that is the difference between a strategy
it can use and one it cannot, and it links working capital management directly to
the solvency objective.

**Efficiency and liquidity pull against each other here more than anywhere
else.** Efficiency means maximum profit from the lowest possible level of
assets. Current assets are assets. So every improvement in efficiency thins the
liquidity buffer, and every increase in the buffer worsens efficiency. Working
capital management is the name for choosing a point on that line.

The topic also reaches into operations (inventory levels are the same decision
seen twice), marketing (credit terms are part of the offer to customers), and
ethics (delaying payment to suppliers is a liquidity strategy whose cost falls on
smaller businesses).

## Terminology (SOURCE)

- **working capital** — funds available for short-term financial commitments
- **net working capital** — current assets minus current liabilities
- **current assets** — assets convertible to cash within twelve months, usually
  cash, receivables and inventory
- **current liabilities** — commitments repayable in the short term, usually
  payables and overdraft
- **working capital cycle** — the time from buying inventory to receiving cash
  for the sale
- **current (working capital) ratio** — current assets divided by current
  liabilities
- **marketable securities** — readily saleable investments held as a liquidity
  reserve
- **receivables / debtors** — money owed to the business by customers
- **payables / creditors** — money the business owes suppliers
- **trade credit** — a supplier's interest-free period before payment is due;
  cost-free finance
- **aged debtors report** — a listing of debtors by how overdue their payment is
- **bad debt** — a receivable that will not be collected
- **floor plan finance** — a supplier provides goods for a period before payment
  is due
- **consignment finance** — payment is not required until goods sell, and unsold
  goods may be returned
- **overdraft** — a facility permitting an account to be overdrawn to a set limit
- **leasing** — paying for the use of an asset owned by another party
- **lessor / lessee** — the owner of the leased asset, and its user
- **sale and lease-back** — selling an owned asset and leasing it back

## Distinctions and confusions

- **Working capital is not cash.** It is current assets minus current
  liabilities, of which cash is one part. A business can have high working
  capital and no cash, if it is all tied up in inventory and receivables.
- **More working capital is not better.** Excess means assets earning less than
  they cost to finance. Both too much and too little cause failure.
- **Receivables are not revenue.** The sale is already recorded. Receivables are
  the cash that has not arrived yet, which is why a profitable business can be
  illiquid.
- **Trade credit is not free money, but it is free finance.** Using the full
  period costs nothing; exceeding it costs charges and future access.
- **A tight credit policy has a cost.** Customers may go to a competitor with
  easier terms. This is the trade students omit when they recommend tightening
  collection.
- **Leasing is not cheaper than buying.** It is cheaper *now*. Over the asset's
  life it generally costs more, and it is chosen for liquidity, flexibility and
  obsolescence protection rather than for total cost.
- **Sale and lease-back is not a sale.** The business keeps using the asset. What
  changes is ownership and the pattern of payment.
- **The current ratio is a measure, not a strategy.** It reports the position the
  strategies produced.

## Instructional implications (USE)

1. name the working capital position: which current asset or liability is the
   problem, and what the cycle length is
2. name the control or strategy chosen
3. state the mechanism: what converts to cash sooner, or what outflow moves
   later, and by how long
4. name the objective it costs: profitability through discount or lease premium,
   efficiency through a larger buffer, a customer relationship through tighter
   terms
5. judge whether it suited this business, and use the cycle length to justify it

Step 5 is where the answer stops being generic. A strategy right for a
manufacturer with a ninety-day cycle is unnecessary for a food outlet whose cycle
is days, and saying that is worth more than any ratio.
