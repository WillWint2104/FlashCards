## Concepts (SOURCE)

**Indicators** are performance measures used to evaluate the effectiveness of a
business, team or individual. The syllabus names seven:

| indicator | what it measures |
| --- | --- |
| **corporate culture** | the values, ideas, expectations and beliefs shared by members of the business |
| **benchmarking key variables** | performance compared against other businesses, other divisions, or best practice |
| **changes in staff turnover** | separation of employees, voluntary and involuntary |
| **absenteeism** | workers not attending when scheduled |
| **accidents** | work-related injury and illness |
| **levels of disputation** | industrial conflict, overt and covert |
| **worker satisfaction** | how employees judge their work and workplace |

Two supporting tools: the **human resource audit**, a diagnostic used to evaluate
HR policies and practices in order to identify problems and develop solutions;
and the distinction between **quantitative** measures, which show the effect in
economic terms, and **qualitative** evaluation, which uses detailed feedback and
research to judge changes in behaviour or quality of service.

## Explanation (SOURCE)

**Corporate culture** is the shared values, ideas, expectations and beliefs. The
chapter approaches it from the employee's side: fair and honest treatment, being
trusted, work appreciated, praise for work done well, a sense of self-worth
through respect, constructive criticism, conflicts handled sensitively, a
harmonious atmosphere, and being part of a team working to a common goal.

The indicators of a *poor* culture are the other six effectiveness measures: high
staff turnover, poor customer service, high absenteeism, accidents, disputes and
internal conflict. Those appear as poor business performance, lower sales, lower
profits than competitors, and ultimately the bottom line.

Features common to the best-rated Australian workplaces: a culture of trust with
transparency, impartiality and equality; collaboration across all levels and
involvement in decision making; high levels of training and mentoring; flexible
and family-friendly practices; high quality personal relationships; pay above
basic rates with some share options; creative perks especially health and
training; and a fun atmosphere.

The chapter's framing claim is that businesses successful in the long term
maintain a balance between concern for success, meaning expansion or profit, and
regard for their employees.

**Benchmarking** compares performance in specific areas against other similar
businesses, other divisions, or best-practice businesses, in order to initiate
improvement. It may follow a full human resource audit or use basic indicators,
and it may involve line management, the HR manager and external consultants.
Four forms:

- **informal** — networking, discussions with colleagues in other businesses,
  visits, online research, conferences
- **performance** — comparing the performance level of a process or activity with
  other businesses
- **best practice** — comparing with a best-practice business in specific areas,
  using a structured process to gain skills and knowledge and modify processes
- **balanced scorecard** — measuring whether the business's activities are meeting
  its established objectives

**Staff turnover** is the separation of employees, voluntary and involuntary,
through dismissal or retrenchment, usually expressed as a percentage of total
staff. Australian turnover averages around 12 to 15 per cent a year and
fluctuates with the economic cycle, with roughly half voluntary through
resignation and retirement. It varies enormously by industry, and hospitality and
hotels commonly run at around 90 per cent a year.

The chapter is explicit about how to assess it: benchmark against other
businesses in the same industry, and determine the *type* of staff leaving and
their reasons, distinguishing **pull** factors outside the business from **push**
factors inside it.

Turnover is costed. The worked example separates the cost of separation from the
cost of replacement and training, with lost productivity and training of new
employees as the largest components, reaching a six-figure total for a single
role.

**Absenteeism** is a worker not turning up when scheduled. High absenteeism or
lateness may indicate dissatisfaction or workplace conflict. The chapter states
that such unofficial expressions of conflict may cost more in lost revenue than
official ones, because the business must carry higher staffing levels to cope,
work is disrupted, productivity falls and labour costs rise. Australian averages
are given at around nine days of unplanned leave per person per year, with a
national annual cost figure attached, and a per-day cost per absent employee. The
recommended response is to find out why people take time off, through meetings or
a suggestion box, and to agree a clear absence policy with staff.

**Accidents.** A stated percentage of Australian employees experience a
work-related injury or illness each year, with a national count of workers
compensation claims for serious injury involving a week or more off work,
permanent incapacity or fatality, a median time lost per serious claim, and the
occupations with the highest and lowest rates. The chapter refers to the
**iceberg effect**: the visible costs of an accident sit above a much larger body
of hidden ones.

**Levels of disputation.** Strikes and lockouts are the officially recorded
forms, but the chapter's most useful contribution is the **overt and covert**
split:

| | by employees | by management |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **overt** | pickets, strikes, stop-work meetings, work bans and boycotts, work-to-rule | lockouts, stand-downs, dismissals, retrenchments |
| **covert** | absenteeism, high turnover, theft and sabotage, higher defect rates, reduced productivity, lack of cooperation | |

**Worker satisfaction** is assessed through feedback and research, and is the
qualitative counterpart to the quantitative indicators.

## Load-bearing derived claims (DERIVED)

```
SOURCE  The chapter lists absenteeism, turnover, theft, higher defect rates,
        reduced productivity and lack of cooperation as covert manifestations of
        industrial conflict. Elsewhere the same items are listed as separate
        effectiveness indicators in their own right.
DERIVED The seven indicators are not seven independent measures. Five of them are
        the same underlying dissatisfaction surfacing in different places, which is
        why they move together. A business with rising absenteeism, rising turnover
        and rising defect rates does not have three problems.
USE     The single most valuable move in an hr-01 answer: read the indicators as a
        pattern rather than a list, and diagnose the cause behind them instead of
        proposing a separate fix for each.
```

```
SOURCE  Turnover in Australia averages 12 to 15 per cent, and hospitality and hotels
        commonly run at around 90 per cent. Benchmark against businesses in the same
        industry, and determine the type of staff leaving and their reasons.
DERIVED A turnover figure is meaningless without its industry benchmark, so a high
        raw number is not evidence of poor HR management and a low one is not
        evidence of good management. The judgement is the gap from the benchmark and
        the composition of who left, not the level.
USE     Turns "turnover was high, therefore HRM was ineffective" into a real
        argument, and it is the same control-for-the-market logic that market share
        analysis provides in marketing.
```

```
SOURCE  A worked example costs a single separation in the six figures, with lost
        productivity and training of the replacement as the largest components.
DERIVED That figure is what converts retention from a welfare argument into a
        financial one, and it is what makes non-monetary rewards and flexible
        rostering economically rational rather than generous.
USE     The bridge between HR and finance in an evaluate answer: a strategy that cost
        money and cut turnover can be judged against the cost of the turnover it
        prevented.
```

```
SOURCE  Covert conflict may cost more in lost revenue than official conflict,
        because the business must carry higher staffing levels, work is disrupted,
        productivity falls and labour costs rise.
DERIVED Industrial dispute statistics therefore understate conflict, and a business
        with no recorded disputes may have more conflict than one with a strike,
        not less.
USE     A precision correction for any answer that uses days lost to industrial
        action as the measure of workplace relations.
```

```
SOURCE  A case describes a business with high turnover concentrated around school
        holidays in a predominantly female workforce, which consulted staff and
        introduced job sharing, shorter night shifts, holiday programs, childcare
        arrangements, banked leave and flexible rostering, after which turnover fell
        below the industry average and return-to-work rates after maternity leave
        rose.
DERIVED This is the only complete chain in the chapter: indicator identified, cause
        diagnosed through consultation, strategy matched to the cause, indicator
        remeasured. The diagnosis step is what made the strategy fit; a generic
        retention bonus would not have addressed a school-holiday pattern.
USE     The model structure for an hr-01 evaluate paragraph, and proof that the
        indicator has to be interpreted before a strategy is chosen.
```

## Relationships and mechanisms (DERIVED unless marked)

This chapter is the **effect** end of hr-01 and the strategies chapter is the
cause end. `evaluate the effectiveness of human resource strategies in improving
business performance` needs three things, and only the middle one is usually
supplied by students:

```
strategy  ->  mechanism  ->  indicator that moved  ->  business performance
```

Which indicator tests which strategy:

| strategy | indicator that actually tests it | why the obvious one misleads |
| --- | --- | --- |
| job design | satisfaction, absenteeism, defect rates | productivity rises with specialisation even where motivation falls |
| training and development | turnover, defect rates, accidents | training cost is immediate and its effect is lagged |
| recruitment, internal | turnover, time to productivity | a good hire shows up as an absence of problems |
| rewards | turnover, satisfaction | pay satisfaction and job satisfaction are different things |
| leadership style | absenteeism, disputation, turnover | output can hold while all three deteriorate |
| dispute resolution | levels of disputation, both overt and covert | overt measures alone can improve while covert conflict rises |
| global strategies | labour cost per unit, and reputational measures | cost falls immediately and other effects arrive later |

**Corporate culture is not a seventh indicator beside the others, it is what the
others measure.** The chapter says as much when it lists high turnover,
absenteeism, accidents and disputes as the indicators of a poor culture, and then
lists each of them separately as an indicator in its own right. Treating culture
as the underlying variable and the rest as its symptoms makes the whole topic
coherent.

**The indicators lag the strategies, and by different amounts.** Absenteeism
responds within weeks, turnover within months, and accident rates and culture
over years. An evaluation conducted too early will find a strategy ineffective
that has not yet had time to work, which is a legitimate limitation to name.

**Quantitative and qualitative are not a ranking.** Quantitative shows the effect
in economic terms and cannot say why. Qualitative says why and cannot size it.
The turnover case in the chapter needed both: the rate identified the problem and
the consultation identified the cause.

**Benchmarking is what makes any of the indicators interpretable**, and this is
the link back to the strategies chapter. Every indicator here is a number without
meaning until compared, either against the same business over time, another
division, the industry, or best practice.

## Trade-offs and conditions (DERIVED)

- **Measuring what is easy against measuring what matters.** Turnover,
  absenteeism and accidents are countable; culture and satisfaction are not.
  Businesses drift toward the countable ones, and those are symptoms.
- **Reducing an indicator against fixing the cause.** Absenteeism can be lowered
  by tightening leave policy without changing why people stay away, which moves
  the problem into a covert form such as presenteeism or reduced cooperation.
- **Benchmarking against best practice can mislead.** A practice that works in a
  business with different job design, workforce and pay structure may not
  transfer, which is the same adaptation problem scanning and learning has in
  operations.
- **The audit costs and delays.** A full human resource audit is thorough and
  slow; basic indicators are fast and shallow. The choice depends on whether the
  problem is known.

## Terminology (SOURCE)

- **indicators** — performance measures used to evaluate effectiveness
- **corporate culture** — the values, ideas, expectations and beliefs shared by
  members of a business
- **benchmarking** — comparing performance between internal sections or between
  businesses
- **informal / performance / best practice / balanced scorecard benchmarking** —
  the four forms
- **human resource audit** — a diagnostic tool evaluating HR policies and
  practices to identify problems and develop solutions
- **staff turnover** — separation of employees, voluntary and involuntary
- **push and pull factors** — reasons for leaving originating inside, or outside,
  the business
- **absenteeism** — failing to attend work when scheduled
- **iceberg effect** — the hidden costs of workplace accidents beneath the visible
  ones
- **overt and covert manifestations** — open industrial action, against concealed
  expressions such as absenteeism, sabotage, defect rates and non-cooperation
- **work-to-rule** — working strictly to the letter of the job description as a
  form of action
- **stand-down** — management directing employees not to work without pay
- **quantitative / qualitative evaluation** — measuring the effect in economic
  terms, against judging change in behaviour or service quality

## Distinctions and confusions

- **SOURCE. Turnover includes both voluntary and involuntary separation.**
  Resignation and retirement sit alongside dismissal and retrenchment.
- **SOURCE. Absenteeism is a covert form of industrial conflict**, not only a
  discipline problem. The chapter classifies it that way explicitly.
- **DERIVED. An indicator is not an objective.** Low turnover is a measure, not a
  goal. A business can achieve zero turnover by retaining people it should have
  let go.
- **DERIVED. A high indicator is not automatically bad.** Industry benchmarks
  differ by a factor of six between the national average and hospitality. The
  number means nothing unbenchmarked.
- **DERIVED. Corporate culture is not perks.** The best-workplace list includes
  perks and also trust, transparency, impartiality and involvement in decisions.
  A business can buy the first and not the second.
- **DERIVED. Effectiveness is not satisfaction.** hr-01 asks about improving
  business performance. A strategy that raised satisfaction without reaching
  productivity, quality, cost or retention has not answered the question.
- **DERIVED. No recorded disputes does not mean no conflict.** Covert forms are
  not counted in dispute statistics.

## Instructional implications (USE)

Paragraph shape for hr-01:

1. name the strategy and the position taken within it
2. name the indicator that would show whether it worked, and say why that
   indicator and not the obvious one
3. give the benchmark, so the number can be read
4. state the mechanism from strategy to indicator
5. give the movement in the indicator, and the lag
6. connect the indicator to business performance in cost or output terms
7. judge, naming what the strategy cost and whether the indicator moved enough to
   justify it

Steps 3 and 6 are where marks are lost. A student who names an indicator without
a benchmark has produced a number nobody can read, and one who stops at the
indicator has evaluated the HR function rather than its contribution to business
performance, which is what the question asks.

Candidate pathways: flexible rostering to turnover, benchmarked against industry,
costed against the separation cost; job design to absenteeism and defect rates;
training to accident rates with a lag; leadership style to covert disputation;
internal recruitment to turnover and time-to-productivity.

## Source gaps

- The seven indicators are presented as a list, and the chapter's own covert
  conflict table shows five of them are the same phenomenon. It never says so.
- No treatment of lag. Every indicator is presented as though it responds
  immediately.
- Corporate culture is defined and then measured only through the other
  indicators, so there is no independent way to assess it.
- No account of an indicator being improved without the underlying cause
  changing, which is the most common real failure.
- Benchmarking is given four forms and no guidance on choosing between them.
- The national averages, cost-per-absence figures, injury statistics and the
  turnover case percentages are quoted from named bodies but without citations a
  student could use. Background for authoring, not evidence.
